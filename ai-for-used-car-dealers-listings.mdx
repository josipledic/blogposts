Picture an independent dealer with thirty-five cars on the lot. One of them is a five-year-old compact hatchback, bought at auction, cleaned, photographed in a hurry on a cloudy Thursday. The listing has eight photos and three lines: "Well kept, full service history, test drive welcome, price negotiable."

A buyer who is seriously interested has four questions. Is it accident-free? Has the cambelt been done? How many keys come with it? Is the winter set included? None of the answers are in the listing, so the buyer does what buyers do: opens the next tab. The hatchback gets 400 views and two messages, and one of the messages is "still available?"

Nothing is wrong with the car. The listing is thin, and thin listings sit. That's the part of a used car business where I think AI earns its keep: turning the facts you already have into a complete, honest listing, and answering the first questions so the salesperson only talks to people who are actually coming.

Those are German numbers, but the pattern holds in most used car markets: independents sell a large share of the cars, and the average car waits months for a buyer. The waiting is not free.

## What a standing day actually costs

Dealers call it *Standkosten*, and it isn't just interest on the loan. It includes financing, marketplace fees, photos, cleaning, moving cars around, and repairing the damage that comes from sitting (flat batteries, flat spots on tyres). According to DAT's dealer barometer, as reported by kfz-betrieb, the average was about [30 euros per car per day in autumn 2024](https://www.kfz-betrieb.vogel.de/dat-barometer-standtage-a-a1968a8a0e4cd2f5d153fe7df75b3e7d/), up roughly 30% from a year before.

Your own figure will differ, and you should calculate it. But even a modest number makes the point:

## Why listings end up thin

It's rarely laziness. The facts are scattered: the equipment list sits in the VIN decoder, the inspection notes are on a workshop job card or a WhatsApp message, the service history is a folder of scans, and the salesperson who took the car in knows the story but is out with a customer. Writing a good listing means collecting all that, and it happens at 6 p.m. when the day is over.

Thin listings also make a legal problem worse. If a listing says nothing about condition, the buyer will ask, and the answer will come from someone's memory on the phone. Claims made from memory are where trouble starts, as we'll see below.

## The blueprint: facts in, draft out, dealer signs off

The setup I'd build for a small dealer has one rule: the AI writes from a fact sheet, never from imagination. Everything it says about the car must trace back to a field in the fact sheet. A person approves every claim about condition before anything is published.

<Flow title="From a car on the lot to a live listing" caption="The fact sheet is the source of truth. The model drafts and checks; a named person approves the statements about condition.">
<Step title="Build the fact sheet" who="Dealer software and AI" tone="ai" time="10 min">VIN data (model, engine, gearbox, first registration), equipment from the decoder, mileage, number of owners, keys, service records and the inspection notes, typed or spoken by whoever checked the car in.</Step>
<Step title="Draft the listing" who="AI" tone="ai" time="1 min">A headline, a short summary, equipment grouped by what buyers care about, condition notes in plain words, what's included, and what the car is not (for example, a scratch on the rear bumper).</Step>
<Step title="Flag what doesn't add up" who="AI" tone="ai" time="1 min">Text vs data: automatic gearbox in the text, manual in the VIN data; mileage lower than the last service entry; 'accident-free' with no record of how that was checked.</Step>
<Step title="Approve the claims" who="Salesperson or owner" tone="human" time="3 min">A person reads the condition statements, fixes or removes anything they can't stand behind, and confirms accident history, mileage and previous owners.</Step>
<Step title="Publish and answer" who="AI and salesperson" time="same day">The listing goes to your marketplaces and website. An assistant answers the first questions from the approved fact sheet and books the viewing.</Step>
<Step title="Review at day 14 and 30" who="Dealer" tone="human">Views, enquiries, viewings. Low views mean photos or price; views without enquiries mean the listing or the price; enquiries without sales mean the car.</Step>
</Flow>

The fact sheet does most of the work. A model writes a decent listing from a clean fact sheet and a poor one from a vague memory. So the boring part, getting the inspection notes down at intake while the car is in front of you, is where the time is saved.

## What a draft looks like

Here's the kind of output I'd expect for a hypothetical car, with the flags a person sees before publishing:

```text
DRAFT: 2019 compact hatchback, 1.5 petrol, manual, 5 doors
78,400 km, 2 previous owners, full service history (7 entries), 2 keys
Equipment: parking sensors front and rear, heated front seats, adaptive cruise control,
navigation, alloy wheels 17", second set of winter tyres on steel wheels included
Condition: new brake pads and discs front (March 2026), cambelt changed at 60,000 km.
Small scratch on rear bumper (photo 14). Interior: light wear on driver's seat bolster.
Inspection: passed, valid until 05/2028.

FLAGS FOR REVIEW
1. Text says "adaptive cruise control"; VIN equipment list shows standard cruise control. Check.
2. Intake notes say "no accident known"; no documented basis. Draft says "no accident known to us",
   not "accident-free". Decide wording.
3. Service entry 7 shows 79,100 km; mileage field says 78,400. Check odometer and records.
```

Each flag is a question only a person can answer. Flag 2 is the one I'd care about most, and it leads to the legal side.

## Where listings go wrong legally

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice, but a few rules shape how this should be built. I'll use German law as the main example, with the UK and US in the next section.

<Callout title="'Accident-free' is a promise, not a mood" tone="warning">
In Germany, a dealer who states that a car is accident-free without having checked, "into the blue", can be treated as acting fraudulently if the statement is wrong, according to case law of the Federal Court of Justice (BGH) summarised by the [Kfz-Innung Schleswig-Holstein](https://www.kfz-sh.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Sales/U-126a-2025_Unfallfahrzeuge_2025-1.pdf). The practical rule for the assistant: it may only repeat accident statements that a person has approved, and it writes "no accident known to us" unless you can document the check.
</Callout>

A few more points from German consumer law:

- **Warranty period.** Dealers owe consumers statutory warranty (*Sachmängelhaftung*). For used goods the period can be shortened to one year only if the buyer is told separately before signing and the shortening is agreed expressly and separately, under [§ 476 BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__476.html). There's also a legal debate about whether shortening the limitation period fits EU law, following the ECJ's Ferenschild decision, so have your purchase contract text checked.
- **Burden of proof.** Under [§ 477 BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__477.html), a defect that appears within a year of handover is presumed to have existed at handover, and the dealer has to prove otherwise. A detailed, honest condition description at the time of sale helps you here, because it shows what state the car was in.
- **Prices.** Consumers must see the total price. If you sell under differential taxation (*Differenzbesteuerung*, § 25a UStG), VAT isn't shown separately, as [this overview for dealers](https://www.anwalt.de/rechtstipps/differenzbesteuerung-beim-gebrauchtwagenhandel_167977.html) explains. The assistant must never write "VAT deductible" on such a car.

### Other countries

The UK's Consumer Rights Act gives a buyer a [short-term right to reject](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/22) for 30 days, and a fault appearing within six months is presumed to have been there at delivery. In the US, the FTC's [Used Car Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/used-car-rule) requires dealers to display a Buyers Guide on each used car, stating whether a warranty is offered and its terms. A listing that contradicts what's on the Buyers Guide is asking for a dispute.

## Answering the first buyer questions

The other half of the setup is the inbox. A typical dealer gets questions like "Is it still available?", "Can you do a better price?", "Is the service history complete?", "Do you deliver?" Most are answered by the fact sheet, and the rest need a person.

The rule for the assistant is the same as for the listing: answer only from the approved fact sheet, and hand over everything else. The things it never decides:

<Split title="Who does what in the inbox" leftTitle="The assistant does" rightTitle="The assistant never does" left={[

  "Sends extra photos and the service history summary you've approved",
  "Offers viewing and test drive slots from your calendar",
  "Collects name, phone number and what the buyer is looking for",
  "Tells the salesperson which enquiries are serious, with a two-line summary"
]} right={[
  "Agrees a price or promises a discount",
  "Makes any statement about accidents, repairs or condition that isn't in the approved sheet",
  "Promises a warranty or financing terms",
  "Accepts a trade-in value or takes a deposit",
  "Pretends to be a person"
]} />

That last line has a legal edge. From 2 August 2026, under [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/), people interacting with an AI system have to be told so, unless it's obvious. A one-line opener covers it: "I'm the dealership's automated assistant. A colleague will take it from here if you want to book a viewing."

Buyer messages contain names, phone numbers and sometimes trade-in details, so they're personal data. In the EU that means a data processing agreement with the provider, a clear privacy notice on your site, and keeping the conversations in a system you control. The UK has its own rules under UK GDPR with the same basic demands.

## When you don't need AI

Four things usually beat a model on cost.

**A photo routine.** Twenty-odd photos in the same order, taken on the same spot in decent light, plus the dashboard, the tyres, the damage. Buyers trust what they can see, and no text can replace a missing photo of the driver's seat.

**A listing template.** If your dealer software can export the fields into a fixed text structure, a template already gives you a decent baseline. AI's extra value is in the condition notes and the flags, not in a nicer adjective.

**A price rule.** Review every car at day 30 and day 60, compare it with similar listings, and move the price by a fixed step. It's a decision, not a writing task, and it's probably the biggest lever on standing days.

**A standing-days report.** A weekly list of cars over 45 days, reviewed at a fifteen-minute meeting, is free and works.

I'd do these first. The AI drafting helps when you have enough cars that the writing is a bottleneck, or when the same facts need to appear consistently on your website, marketplaces and social posts.

## Tools that fit

The building blocks are already part of most dealers' setup: dealer management software or a stock system, the marketplace upload interface (mobile.de, AutoScout24 or Autotrader, depending on the country), a VIN decoder or valuation service such as DAT, and a shared inbox or messenger. The AI sits between them, reads the fact sheet and writes the draft into the field your stock system already has. It doesn't replace the stock system, which stays the record of the car.

Start with one car type, for example the ten cheapest cars on the lot, where a bad listing is least costly. Compare views and enquiries per car with the last ten similar ones.

## Questions used car dealers ask

### Can AI write my used car listings?

Yes, from a fact sheet, and it should be a person who approves the statements about condition. The model is good at structure and readable wording. It's bad at knowing what you didn't tell it, so a model that's missing the inspection notes fills the gap with plausible words, which is exactly what you don't want in a car listing.

### Am I liable for what the AI writes?

Yes. To a buyer, a statement in your listing is your statement. That's why the setup above has a named person approve every claim about condition, and why the assistant is limited to the approved fact sheet.

### What should a good used car listing contain?

The facts buyers ask about first: mileage, first registration, number of owners, service history, accident statement you can stand behind, inspection validity, keys, equipment and wheels, what's included, and what's not perfect. Plenty of photos, including the flaws.

### Do I need to tell customers the chat assistant is an AI?

In the EU, yes, from 2 August 2026 under Article 50 of the AI Act, unless it's obvious from the context. I'd do it everywhere, since it costs nothing and avoids a bad surprise.

## My rule of thumb

Write the listing you'd want to read if you were buying the car, from facts you can prove, and let a person sign off on anything about accidents, mileage and owners. Use AI for the first draft and the inconsistency check, and keep price decisions with you.
