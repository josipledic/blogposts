Start with the numbers, because in a salon they're unforgiving.

UK hair and beauty businesses told a booking platform's survey that they lose almost 7% of their monthly revenue to cancellations and no-shows. The industry's own federation found that one in five hair and beauty businesses is operating at a loss, and fewer than one in ten is making what owners call a good profit. When margins are that thin, a 90-minute colour appointment that cancels at 9 a.m. for 11 a.m. isn't a small annoyance. It can be the day's profit.

Most salons respond in one of two ways. They accept the gap and hope for a walk-in. Or someone grabs the phone and sends a message to every client in the book: "Last-minute slot at 11, 20% off!" The first loses money. The second loses something harder to get back: clients who start ignoring your messages, clients who wait for discounts, and in some countries, a complaint for unsolicited marketing.

There's a better middle path. Offer the gap to the handful of clients who actually want it, in a message that feels like service rather than advertising. That's a matching problem, and matching is something AI does well.

The point of the calculation isn't the exact figure. It's that most of it is profit: the stylist is already paid for that hour. That's also why it's worth doing carefully rather than aggressively. A gap filled by annoying ten clients is not a win.

## Who should hear about the gap

Everyone on your client list is not your audience for a last-minute slot. A few people are:

- **The waitlist.** Clients who asked for an earlier appointment with this stylist, and said which days and times work.
- **Clients who are due.** The client who comes every six weeks for a colour and is now at seven, the one who books a cut every month and hasn't yet.
- **Clients who said yes to last-minute offers.** People who opted in, told you when they're usually free and how much notice they need.
- **Clients who fit the slot.** The gap is 90 minutes with a colour-trained stylist; offering it to someone who needs a two-and-a-half-hour balayage helps nobody.

Putting those lists together by hand at 9 a.m., while the phone rings and the first client of the day is in the chair, is exactly what doesn't happen. A model connected to your booking system can do it in seconds.

## A waitlist worth having

Most salon waitlists are a name and a phone number on a sticky note, or a feature in the booking app nobody uses. A useful one captures a little more, at the moment the client asks:

- **Which stylist, and whether another one is fine.** Many clients will only see their own stylist; some just want the service.
- **Which service.** A cut is 45 minutes, a colour correction is four hours. The gap has to fit.
- **When they can come.** Days, times, and how much notice they need. "Weekday mornings, if I hear by 9" is gold.
- **Until when.** A request for an earlier slot before a wedding expires after the wedding.
- **How to reach them.** Their preferred channel, and their agreement to be contacted about openings.

When a client asks at the desk or in a message for an earlier appointment, the model can ask these questions, record the answers and confirm them back. Then, when a gap opens, the right people are already known.

The messages matter as much as the matching. A good one sounds like the front desk, not a promotion:

> Hi Laura, Mia has had a cancellation this morning at 11:00, and you mentioned you'd like to come in before your holiday. It's enough time for your usual colour and cut. Would you like it? Just reply yes and it's yours.

Compare that with "LAST MINUTE SLOT 11AM 20% OFF!!" sent to 800 people. One is a favour. The other is noise.

## Service, or spam?

<Split title="Two ways to fill a gap" leftTitle="Spam" rightTitle="Service" left={["Blast to the whole client list", "Generic discount to make it move", "Several offers a week to the same people", "Everyone, whether they agreed or not", "Clients learn to wait for the discount"]} right={["A personal message to the few who fit", "The service they already book, at the usual price", "At most one last-minute offer per client per week", "Only clients who opted in or asked to be on the waitlist", "Clients learn that asking for an earlier slot works"]} caption="Targeted offers usually fill gaps without discounts, because the people receiving them already wanted an appointment." />

The legal side points the same way. In the UK, marketing texts and emails need consent or fall under the "soft opt-in" for existing customers, with an easy way to unsubscribe. In the US, marketing texts generally need prior express written consent under the TCPA. A message to a client on your waitlist who asked for an earlier appointment is service. A discount blast to everyone who ever booked is marketing, with all the rules that come with it. Ask your adviser where the line sits for you, and keep opt-ins recorded in your booking system.

## Fewer gaps in the first place

Filling gaps is the second-best outcome. Not having them is better, and several simple things help, AI or not:

**Reminders that make rescheduling easy.** A reminder two days before and on the morning, with a link to move the appointment, turns many no-shows into reschedules with enough notice to refill the slot. Most booking systems do this without any AI.

**Deposits where they make sense.** For long services, new clients booking online and repeat no-shows, a deposit or card on file changes behaviour. Put the policy on your booking page and in the confirmation.

**Conversations the model can handle.** Clients reply to reminders in free text: "Can we make it 30 minutes later?", "My daughter is ill, can I bring her next week instead?" A model can read those replies, check the book and offer options, which saves the front desk a stream of back-and-forth messages. Anything unusual goes to a person.

**Rebooking before they leave.** The easiest appointment to fill is the next one, booked at the till. A prompt at checkout ("Laura usually comes every six weeks: suggest 14 November with Mia?") keeps regulars regular.

## What stays with people

- **Discounts and exceptions.** Whether to offer a price cut for a gap, waive a cancellation fee, or squeeze in a longer service is the salon's decision.
- **Difficult conversations.** A client upset about a charge, a complaint about a result, a sensitive request: a person, always.
- **Knowing your clients.** The model knows booking history. The stylist knows that Laura hates early mornings, whatever the data says.

## When you don't need AI

A one-chair salon with a waitlist feature in its booking app doesn't need a model. Turn on automatic reminders, keep a short waitlist, and message the one or two people on it personally. AI starts paying off with several stylists, a busy book, and a front desk too stretched to work through lists when a cancellation comes in.

## Tools that fit

Salon software such as Fresha, Phorest, Timely, Vagaro or Boulevard already handles online booking, reminders, deposits and often waitlists. The AI layer works with that data: it matches gaps to the right clients, writes personal offers, reads free-text replies, and suggests rebooking at checkout. ## Questions salon owners ask

### Should we discount last-minute slots?

Usually not. Targeted offers to clients who wanted an appointment anyway fill most gaps at full price. Regular discounts teach clients to wait. If you want an incentive, a small extra (a treatment or a product sample) protects your prices better than a percentage.

### Are no-show fees worth it?

For repeat no-shows and long services, a clear cancellation policy with a fee or deposit usually reduces the problem. It has to be agreed at booking, visible on your booking page and applied consistently. The model can explain the policy and remind clients of it; deciding to charge or waive a fee stays with the salon.

### How many clients should get the first message?

Start with three and see. Too many, and several people reply yes to a slot that's already gone, which is annoying for them. Too few, and the slot may stay empty. The "thanks, it's taken" message matters as much as the offer.

### Can the model handle Instagram and WhatsApp messages too?

Often, through the platforms' business tools, but keep bookings in one system. The model reads messages from any channel and books in the salon software, so there's one calendar, not three.

### Will clients know it's an assistant?

They should, especially in free-text conversations. In the EU, [Article 50 of the AI Act](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) has required AI systems that talk to people to say so since August 2026. A simple sign-off like "Salon assistant, on behalf of the team" does the job.

## Rule of thumb

Know what a gap is worth, prevent the ones you can with reminders and deposits, and fill the rest by offering them to the few clients who want them, personally and at the usual price. Never blast the whole list, and never message anyone who hasn't agreed to hear from you.
