A couple walks into a small travel agency with a printout. "We asked a chatbot to plan two weeks in Japan in April. Can you book this?" The plan looks impressive: day-by-day, with temples, ryokans, a cooking class and a day trip to a famous garden. The agent spends forty minutes checking it. Two of the ryokans don't have rooms for those dates, one doesn't seem to exist under that name, the garden is closed on the day it's scheduled, and the train connection on day nine would leave them at a rural station after the last bus.

That scene is becoming normal, and it says two things about AI in travel. Clients are already using it. And the value of a good agent hasn't disappeared, it has moved: from finding ideas to making a plan true.

This post goes through five common beliefs about AI and travel agencies, what's actually the case, and a practical setup in which AI drafts the itinerary and the agent does what only an agent can.

## Myth 1: "Clients will plan with a chatbot and skip the agent"

Some will, for simple trips they could always book themselves. But the research behind the numbers above also shows that AI users tend to be frequent, higher-spending travelers who combine digital tools with human advice rather than replacing it. The printout in the opening scene is typical: the client used AI for inspiration and came to the agency for certainty.

The reality: the agent's job is shifting from "what could we do?" to "what will actually work, at what price, and who fixes it if it goes wrong?" That's a stronger position than it sounds, because it's exactly what a chatbot can't provide.

## Myth 2: "AI can produce a finished, bookable itinerary"

It can produce something that looks finished. Language models are very good at plausible itineraries and not reliable on the details that make a trip work: opening days, seasonal closures, real travel times between places, whether a hotel has availability or even exists under that name, entry requirements, and what anything costs this season.

The reality: AI is an excellent first-draft writer and a poor source of facts. The draft saves the agent the blank page. Every fact in it still has to be checked against real booking systems, supplier information and the agent's own knowledge.

## Myth 3: "Tailoring needs a long intake conversation every time"

A good intake conversation is valuable, but much of it is collecting facts: dates, budget, who's travelling, pace, interests, mobility, things they've done before and didn't like. Those can be gathered in a short, friendly questionnaire before the conversation, which then focuses on what matters: what the trip is really for.

The reality: a questionnaire plus AI turns scattered answers into a clear travel brief in minutes. The agent reads the brief before the call instead of taking notes during it.

## Myth 4: "Automation makes the service impersonal"

The impersonal part of travel planning is the waiting: three days for a first proposal, another two for a revision. The personal part is the agent who remembers that the client's mother uses a walking stick, knows which hotel in Kyoto has a quiet garden room, and answers the phone when a flight is cancelled.

The reality: a draft in an hour instead of a day gives the agent more time for the parts that are personal, and gets the client a first proposal while they're still excited about the trip.

## Myth 5: "AI doesn't change the legal side"

True, and that's the point. When an agency combines flights, hotels and other services into one trip for a client, it can become the organiser of a package under package travel rules (in the EU, the [Package Travel Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2015/2302/oj)), with liability for the whole trip, information duties and insolvency protection. It doesn't matter whether the itinerary was drafted by a person or a model.

The reality: every AI-drafted itinerary goes through the same checks as any other proposal, including whether it's a package, what that means for liability and insurance, and what the client must be told before booking.

## The setup

A brief produced from the questionnaire might look like this:

> **Travellers:** couple in their early 60s, second trip to Asia, first to Japan. **Dates:** 8 to 22 April, flexible by two days. **Budget:** around €9,000 for both, excluding flights. **Pace:** relaxed; no more than two hotel changes; one free afternoon every few days. **Interests:** gardens, food, crafts; not interested in nightlife or theme parks. **Mobility:** she has a knee problem, avoid long stair climbs and heavy luggage days. **Loved before:** small family-run hotels in Vietnam. **Open questions for the call:** cherry blossom timing versus crowds; one ryokan night or several; rail pass or individual tickets.

That's what the agent reads before the call. The knee problem alone changes half the itinerary: luggage forwarding between cities, hotels with lifts, temples chosen for their paths, not their fame. A model can hold that constraint across every day of the draft, and the agent checks that it did.

The step that matters most is the agent's own knowledge base: preferred hotels and guides, notes from past client feedback, suppliers to avoid, local tips. The more of that the model can draw on, the more the draft sounds like your agency instead of like a generic travel blog.

## The verification checklist

<Checklist title="Before any AI-drafted itinerary goes to a client" items={[
  "Every hotel, tour and transfer checked for existence and availability on the dates",
  "Prices taken from booking systems or suppliers, not from the draft",
  "Opening days, seasonal closures and local holidays checked for each activity",
  "Travel times and connections checked, including last trains and buses",
  "Entry, visa and health requirements checked for the travellers' nationalities",
  "Accessibility and pace checked against what the client told you",
  "Package travel status clarified, with the right information and protection",
  "Travel insurance and cancellation terms explained"
]} />

That list is what the client is paying for. It's also a good way to explain your value when a client arrives with their own AI plan: "I'll check every one of these against real availability and fix what won't work."

## Working with the client's own AI plan

When clients bring their own AI-made itinerary, treat it as a brief, not as competition. It tells you what excites them. Run it through the same checks, keep what works, replace what doesn't, and explain why ("this ryokan is fully booked, but this one is ten minutes away, has a private onsen, and I've had great feedback on it"). Many clients are relieved to hand the details to someone who knows.

Some agencies charge a planning fee for this kind of work, credited against the booking. That's a reasonable response to a real service: turning an attractive plan into a trip that actually works.

## Tools that fit

Agencies work with booking platforms, supplier portals and itinerary tools such as Travefy, Tern or Axus, many of which now include AI features. Your CRM holds client history and preferences. The AI layer for drafting sits alongside: it reads the questionnaire and your knowledge base, drafts the brief and itinerary, and hands them to the agent. Prices and availability always come from the booking systems, never from the model.

Client data includes passport details, health information and family circumstances. Keep it in systems with proper data processing agreements, and don't paste it into consumer chatbots.

## Questions travel agents ask

### Will AI replace travel agents?

For simple, low-value trips, much of that business went online years ago. For complex, high-value and personal trips, the value of an agent who verifies, advises and solves problems is, if anything, clearer now that clients can generate plausible but unreliable plans themselves.

### How do I stop the model from inventing hotels?

Give it your own supplier list and instruct it to use only those or mark new suggestions as "to verify". Even then, check everything. The model should never be the source of truth for anything bookable.

### Can the AI write the final proposal text?

It writes good descriptions and day summaries. Edit them into your voice, and make sure every factual claim in the text matches what you've booked or quoted.

### Is this worth it for a small agency?

It's most useful for tailor-made trips, where drafting takes hours. If most of your business is packaged holidays from tour operators, the gain is smaller and lies more in client communication than in itinerary drafting.

## Rule of thumb

Let AI write the first draft and never let it be the source of a fact. Check everything bookable against real systems, add what only you know, and sell the one thing a chatbot can't offer: a trip that works, and someone to call when it doesn't.
