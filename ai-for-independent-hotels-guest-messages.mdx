At 10:40 at night, a guest who arrives tomorrow sends a message through the booking platform: "Is there parking, and can we check in before 3? We're coming with our dog." The night porter at a 28-room independent hotel is also handling a late arrival and a broken key card. The message waits until the morning shift, which answers it at 8:15, between breakfast orders and three checkouts. By then the guest has sent a second message, slightly irritated, and booked a parking garage nearby.

Every independent hotel knows this pattern. The questions are simple and they repeat: parking, check-in, breakfast, pets, directions, late arrival. Each takes a minute to answer, but they arrive at all hours, through five channels, while the same few people run the front desk. This is one of the clearest places where AI helps a small hotel, as long as it knows the hotel's real answers and knows when to hand over to a person.

Here's what the 10:40 p.m. message could have received, within a minute:

> Good evening, and thanks for your message! Yes, we have parking in our courtyard: €12 per night, and I've reserved a space for you. Your room is usually ready from 3 p.m.; tomorrow it looks like we can have it ready by 1 p.m. for a €20 early check-in fee. Would you like that? Dogs are very welcome: we've noted it on your booking and there's a €15 per night charge, water bowl included. If you tell me roughly when you'll arrive, I'll let the team know. This is our automated assistant; for anything else, the front desk will reply in the morning.

Every fact in it comes from the hotel's own information and the booking. The guest sleeps well, the parking is secured, and the hotel has probably sold an early check-in.

## The staffing reality

The American Hotel & Lodging Association's latest survey found that [65% of hotels still report staffing shortages](https://www.ahla.com/news/65-surveyed-hotels-report-staffing-shortages), with front desk roles the second most commonly understaffed after housekeeping. European hotels tell the same story. Guest expectations, meanwhile, point the other way: people who book online expect online answers, quickly, at whatever hour they happen to be planning their trip.

An independent hotel can't staff a 24-hour messaging desk. It doesn't need to, if most questions can be answered correctly the moment they arrive.

## Start by counting

Before setting anything up, look at three months of pre-arrival messages across email, the booking platforms and messaging apps, and sort them. The pattern is usually obvious within an hour.

## The questions, ranked, and what AI can do with each

### 1. Check-in and check-out times

The most frequent question, and a revenue opportunity. The assistant answers with the standard times and, if the property management system shows the room will be ready early, offers early check-in at your price. Late check-out works the same way. This is the one question where a good answer makes money.

### 2. Parking

Is there parking, how much, can I reserve it, how high is the garage, is there a charger for my car? The answers are fixed facts, and an assistant can give them precisely, reserve a space if your system allows it, and explain the alternative when the hotel's spaces are full. It's the question guests most often ask twice when nobody answers, because they need to plan the drive.

### 3. Late arrival and key pickup

"We'll arrive at midnight, is that okay?" needs an answer tied to your process: key safe, door code, night porter, self check-in. The assistant can answer and, just as important, record the expected arrival time in the booking so the night shift knows.

### 4. Breakfast

Times, price, whether it's included, vegan or gluten-free options, breakfast to take away for an early departure. Fixed facts, plus the chance to add breakfast to a room-only booking.

### 5. Directions and transport

From the station, from the airport, where the tram stops, how long a taxi takes. Easy to answer from your own directions page, and much more useful when it's specific to where the guest is coming from.

### 6. Pets

Allowed or not, which rooms, what it costs, what the rules are. A clear, consistent answer avoids the awkward conversation at the desk.

### 7. Restaurant tips and bookings

The assistant can share your own list of recommendations, with notes on which need booking. Making the reservation for the guest is where a person is often better, or a direct booking link.

### 8. Room details

Twin or double, cot available, a bath or a shower, step-free access, a quiet room away from the street. The assistant can answer from room data and note requests in the booking. Requests that can't be guaranteed should be confirmed as requests, not promises.

### 9. Changes and cancellations

Here the assistant should explain the policy of the guest's own booking, not a general one. Anything involving money, exceptions or bookings from platforms with their own rules goes to a person.

### 10. Invoices

Company name and address on the invoice, VAT number, split bills. The assistant can collect the details and add them to the booking; a person checks before the invoice is issued.

## Timing matters as much as answers

The best guest messaging doesn't just react. It sends the right information before the guest has to ask, which removes many questions altogether.

<Timeline title="A guest's messages from booking to review" caption="Each message answers the questions guests usually ask at that point. The assistant handles replies in between, and hands over when a person is needed.">
<Phase when="At booking" title="Confirmation">Booking details, cancellation terms in plain words, and one line about parking and check-in times.</Phase>
<Phase when="7 days before" title="Pre-arrival message">Arrival options, parking reservation, breakfast, pets. A question: "What time do you expect to arrive?"</Phase>
<Phase when="2 days before" title="Practical details">Directions from their likely starting point, early check-in availability, restaurant tips for the arrival evening.</Phase>
<Phase when="Arrival day" title="Access">Door code or self check-in instructions for late arrivals, sent only on the day. A contact for problems.</Phase>
<Phase when="During the stay" title="Available, not intrusive">Answers to questions about breakfast, late check-out, taxis. Problems go straight to the desk.</Phase>
<Phase when="After check-out" title="Thanks and review">A short thank-you with an invoice copy and a review link. Negative feedback goes to the manager first.</Phase>
</Timeline>

The foundation is a well-written set of hotel facts: every answer your front desk gives, written down once. Most hotels have it scattered across the website, the welcome folder and staff memory. Writing it down properly is an afternoon's work, and it helps new staff as much as it helps the assistant.

## Tools that fit

Property management systems such as Mews, Cloudbeds, Apaleo or protel hold the bookings, and many connect to guest messaging platforms that combine all channels into one inbox and add AI replies. Check what your PMS already offers before building anything. The part that decides quality isn't the software, it's the hotel facts it answers from and the rules for when to hand over.

Guest data is personal data: keep it within systems that have a data processing agreement, don't let the assistant repeat details from the booking to anyone who can't prove they're the guest, and in the EU make it clear that guests are chatting with an automated assistant, which [Article 50 of the AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) requires since August 2026.

## Questions hoteliers ask

### Won't guests miss the personal touch?

The personal touch happens at the desk, in the breakfast room and in how problems are solved. Nobody feels personally touched by waiting nine hours for the parking price. Fast, correct answers to routine questions give staff more time for the moments that are actually personal.

### What about booking platform messages?

Most platforms let you connect a messaging tool so replies go back through the platform. Keep their rules in mind, especially around contact details and payments.

### Can it upsell without being pushy?

Yes, if offers are relevant and occasional: early check-in when the guest mentions an early train, parking when they mention driving, breakfast when their rate doesn't include it. One offer at the right moment works better than a list of extras in every message.

### What if it gives a wrong answer?

It will, occasionally, usually because the hotel facts are outdated (breakfast times changed for winter, parking prices went up). A weekly review of handed-over and corrected conversations catches most of it, and updating the facts fixes it for every future guest.

## Rule of thumb

Write down every answer your front desk gives, once. Let the assistant give those answers instantly, send the right information before guests have to ask, and hand every complaint, exception and doubt to a person with a summary.
