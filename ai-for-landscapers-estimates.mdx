The owner of a small landscaping company is excellent at the work. On a Tuesday afternoon, she walks a client's backyard and sees the whole job in ten minutes: rip out the tired lawn strip along the fence, a new planting bed with drought-tolerant perennials, a gravel path to the shed, fix the drainage by the patio, fresh mulch everywhere.

Turning that picture into an estimate takes the rest of the week. Sunday evening, she sits down with her notes, a measuring wheel's worth of numbers, and a price list. Square footage for the bed. Cubic yards of soil and mulch. Linear feet of edging. Plant counts by size. Labour hours for excavation and hauling. Three hours later, she has a number. She sends it Monday. The client thanks her politely and mentions they went with another company that quoted on Wednesday.

The design took ten minutes. The arithmetic took three hours and cost her the job. That's the part I'd hand to software.

On both sides of the Atlantic, the pattern is the same: demand is steady, crews are hard to hire, and most firms are small enough that the owner is also the estimator, the designer, and often the person on the mower. Every hour spent converting square feet into cubic yards is an hour not spent selling, supervising, or resting. And in a market where clients often ask two or three companies, speed matters. The estimate that arrives first sets the frame for the others.

## Why estimates are slow

When I break an estimate down, the time rarely goes to the creative part. It goes to four mechanical steps.

1. **Measuring.** Areas of lawn, beds, and hardscape, lengths of edging and fence, often from rough notes and a wheel.
2. **Converting.** Area times depth into volume; volume into loads or bags; plant spacing into counts.
3. **Assembling.** Knowing that a "new bed" means excavation, haul-away, soil amendment, edging, plants, and mulch, and not forgetting one.
4. **Pricing and writing.** Applying your prices and labour rates, then writing a description a client can understand.

All four are exactly what software is good at, provided it works from your numbers and your standard assemblies.

## Three ways to produce an estimate

| Method | Time per estimate | Strengths | What it misses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Site walk, wheel, and notepad | Hours of evening work | Complete picture of the site | Slow, error-prone arithmetic, forgotten line items |
| Aerial measurement tools | Much faster measuring | Accurate areas for open lots, can be done before the visit | Areas under trees, slopes, soil, drainage, access |
| Site walk plus AI-drafted estimate | Draft ready the same afternoon | Your observations, measured areas, and your assemblies combined | Still needs your review and your judgment on price |

Aerial measurement tools such as Go iLawn, SiteRecon, or the takeoff features in LMN and similar platforms already solve much of the measuring. What they don't capture is what you notice on the walk: the soggy corner, the narrow side gate, the roots, the neighbour's dog. The third row combines both and adds the step most tools leave to you, which is turning it all into a priced, readable proposal.

The model's contribution is narrow but valuable: understanding "new bed along the back fence, six feet deep, full sun, clay" and knowing which of your assemblies that means. The arithmetic afterwards is plain calculation, done by rules, not guessed by a language model.

None of that is hard. It's just tedious, and tedium is where mistakes live: the missing half-yard of mulch that means a second delivery, or the forgotten haul-away line that turns a profitable job into a break-even one.

## What the client reads

The line items are for you. The client reads the description, and that's where a draft saves the most time, because writing clear, friendly prose at 10 p.m. is hard. For the back-fence bed, the draft might say:

> **New planting bed along the back fence (about 40 × 6 ft).** We'll remove the existing lawn strip and haul it away, loosen the clay soil and work in compost so roots can establish, and install steel edging along the lawn side for a clean line. The bed will be planted with drought-tolerant perennials suited to full sun, spaced so they fill in by the second summer, and finished with 3 inches of shredded hardwood mulch to hold moisture and keep weeds down.

That paragraph comes from your voice note, your assembly, and your plant list. You read it, change "shredded hardwood" to whatever you actually use, and move on. Clients understand what they're paying for, which makes the price easier to accept and the conversation shorter.

Plant choices deserve a word of caution. A language model will happily suggest plants, and some of its suggestions will be wrong for your climate zone, your soil, or what your nursery actually stocks. I'd restrict any plant suggestions to your own list of plants you use and can source, with their hardiness and light requirements attached. The model picks from your list; it doesn't invent from its training data.

## What to say during the walk

Voice notes work best when you cover the same things at every site. A short checklist on your phone helps.

<Checklist title="Site walk prompts" items={[
  "Area name and what you want to do there",
  "Sun or shade, and soil type if you can tell",
  "Slope, standing water, or signs of drainage problems",
  "Access for machines: gate width, steps, distance from the street",
  "Existing plants, stumps, or structures to remove and haul away",
  "Irrigation and outdoor lighting, existing or wanted",
  "Utilities: mark it for a locate request before any digging (811 in the US)",
  "HOA or neighbour constraints the client mentioned"
]} />

The utilities line isn't optional. In the US, calling 811 before you dig is required, and in most of Europe, utility maps have to be requested before excavation. A note on the walk means the request goes out in time, not the morning the crew arrives.

The back-test in the first week is the honest part. If the drafts from your old notes come close to your hand-made estimates, you'll trust the system on new jobs. If they don't, you'll see exactly which assemblies need more detail.

## Questions landscapers ask

### Can AI measure a yard from satellite images?

For open areas, aerial measurement is accurate enough to estimate from, and dedicated tools do it well. Areas under tree canopy, steep slopes, and anything behind structures still need a check on site. That's why the walk stays.

### Will clients notice the estimate was drafted by software?

They'll notice it arrived the same day, reads clearly, and matches what you talked about in their yard. The judgment in it is yours, and you've reviewed every line.

### Can it produce design visuals too?

Image generation can make a quick mood picture, but I'd be careful. A generated image can show plants that won't grow there or a patio that doesn't fit, and clients remember pictures. For anything a client will rely on, use your design software or sketches.

### How do we keep prices current?

Keep one price book, update it when supplier prices change, and let every draft pull from it. The model never decides a price; it looks it up.

## The rule of thumb

Walk every site yourself. Let software do the arithmetic afterwards, so the estimate goes out while the client still remembers your visit.
