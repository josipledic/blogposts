A woman walks into an independent bookshop on a Saturday morning. "It had a blue cover," she says. "Something about a lighthouse. I heard the author on the radio last week." The only bookseller on the floor has a queue at the till and a phone that keeps ringing. She writes "blue book, lighthouse, radio?" on a slip, adds a mobile number, and drops it in the order tray.

That slip waits until Monday. By Monday afternoon someone has found the book, rung a mobile that goes to voicemail, and entered the order two days later than it could have been. The customer has long since typed the same description into a search engine and bought the book from a retailer that promised next-day delivery.

The shop could have ordered that book within minutes on Saturday and had it on the counter at the start of the week. The wholesalers are not the problem, and neither is the price. The problem is the first five minutes of a vague request, and the last message that tells the customer the book is in. That's where I'd use AI.

The supply side is built for exactly this job: a customer asks, the shop orders before the wholesaler's daily cut-off, the book arrives the next morning. In Germany, Libri, which sells this service, says well over 95% of the titles bookshops sell day to day can be supplied by Libri and delivered overnight nationwide, according to its [wholesale page](https://www.libri.de/en/for-traders/wholesale-trade/). Gardners makes a similar pitch in the UK.

Price is the other half of the picture, and it differs by country. Germany has a fixed book price law, so a title costs the same in a village bookshop and on an online platform, as the [Börsenverein explains](https://www.boersenverein.de/beratung-service/recht/buchpreisbindung/). The UK's Net Book Agreement was [struck down in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_Book_Agreement), and the US has no fixed book price either, so shops there compete on price as well.

Either way, the part a shop controls is the experience around the order: how fast it understands what the customer wants, and whether it says when the book is in.

## Where a customer order leaks

Think about what happens to a special order in a shop that's busy and short-staffed. I'd map four points.

| Step | What goes wrong | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A vague request at the counter | Written on a slip and searched later, between other jobs | A day of delay, and some requests never get resolved |
| Entering the order | It misses the wholesaler's cut-off by an hour | The book arrives a day later than it could have |
| Confirming with the customer | Voicemail, a missed call, a second attempt | Staff time, and a customer who gives up |
| The book arrives | It sits on a shelf and nobody calls | Uncollected stock and an unhappy customer |

None of this is a problem with the wholesaler. These are handoffs inside the shop, which makes them good candidates for software. It also means a clever catalogue search alone won't fix them.

## From a half-remembered book to a copy on the counter

Here's the flow I'd build. The assistant takes requests wherever they arrive: a voice message, a web form, a chat widget, or a note typed in at the till.

<Flow title="From a vague request to a reserved copy" caption="The assistant finds candidates and drafts the order. The bookseller decides whenever the match isn't certain.">
<Step title="Request arrives" who="Customer" time="Saturday, 10:42">A voice message, a form or a note from the counter, in the customer's own words.</Step>
<Step title="Narrow it down" who="AI" tone="ai" time="1 to 2 min">Two or three questions: topic, cover, where they heard of it, fiction or not, hardback or paperback, rough date.</Step>
<Step title="Search the catalogue" who="AI" tone="ai" time="seconds">Up to three candidates with cover, author, price and availability, all taken from the catalogue, none from memory.</Step>
<Step title="Check the match" who="Bookseller" tone="human" time="under 1 min">One strong match: approve with a tap. Several plausible ones, or none: the bookseller calls or suggests alternatives.</Step>
<Step title="Customer confirms" who="Customer" time="minutes">A reply to the text, or a yes on the phone, after seeing the cover and title.</Step>
<Step title="Place the order" who="System" time="before the cut-off">The order goes into the shop system with the customer's name and number. The assistant flags anything close to the wholesaler's deadline.</Step>
<Step title="Tell them it's in" who="AI" tone="ai" time="next morning">When the book is booked in, a short message: it's ready, here are the opening hours, how long it will be held.</Step>
</Flow>

A few design decisions do most of the work.

**It searches a catalogue, never its memory.** Language models produce convincing book titles that don't exist. The assistant has to look the book up in the catalogue your wholesaler or shop system provides, show only what it found there, and report the availability the catalogue gives. In Germany the shared reference is the VLB, which lists around 2.9 million products from more than 22,000 publishers, according to [MVB](https://mvb-online.de/marken-und-produkte/vlb). Ask your software vendor what search interface it offers, because the quality of this step depends on it.

**Confidence decides who acts.** A single strong match goes to the bookseller as a one-tap approval. Two or three plausible matches, or none, get a person's judgment and often a short call. That's the human decision point, and it's where the shop's knowledge comes in.

**The cut-off is part of the logic.** Every wholesaler has a daily order deadline, and yours is in your contract or on its portal. The assistant should know it and push approved requests to the front of the queue as it approaches.

## What the bookseller sees

Here's an invented example of what lands on the bookseller's screen. The titles and prices are made up.

```text
REQUEST, Sat 10:42, voice message (transcribed)
"Blue cover, something about a lighthouse, heard the author on the
radio last week. A novel, I think."
Customer: Maria, mobile on file, OK to text: yes

Candidate 1 (strong): The Lamp at Skerry Point, hardback, £16.99, in stock at wholesaler
Candidate 2 (possible): Salt and Signal, paperback, £9.99, in stock
Not in the data: which radio programme, which week.
Wholesaler cut-off today: 16:00. Next step: approve 1, or call Maria.
```

The bookseller may have heard the same programme. She knows what was on the shortlist this week and which book three customers asked about on Thursday. The assistant doesn't, so it hands over the question and leaves the judgment where it belongs.

The recommendation is the reason people walk into a bookshop instead of scrolling a website. I would not automate it. The assistant is for the dull part of the job, so the bookseller has more time for the part customers actually come for.

## When a form and a text message are enough

Not every shop needs AI for this. If most of your customer orders arrive with a title or an ISBN, a web form with those fields and a text message when the book is booked in will fix the most expensive leak: the book on the shelf that nobody mentioned. Many shop systems and wholesaler tools can already send something like it, so check what yours does before buying anything. The same idea works at a [butcher counter with holiday preorders](/posts/ai-for-butchers-preorders): a clear order, a pickup slot, a message when it's ready.

AI earns its place on the vague requests: voice notes and messages that arrive when the shop is busy or closed, from people who don't have a title. If you get two of those a week, a person with a catalogue is fine. If you get twenty, and half arrive on a Saturday, it starts to pay. For online questions about stock and delivery, the logic is close to [pre-sale support in an online shop](/posts/ai-for-ecommerce-support).

What the sum leaves out is the customer who comes back because it worked once, and the staff time you spend on the shop floor instead of chasing slips. That's why I'd start small.

## Customer data and the rules to check

An order record holds a name, a phone number and a title. What someone orders can say a lot about them: a book on a diagnosis, on debt, on a divorce. Keep records only as long as the order needs, limit who can see them, and keep them out of marketing lists. Under GDPR you also need a data processing agreement with whoever runs the assistant, and you should know where they process the data.

**Pickup messages.** The UK's data protection regulator says routine customer service messages, such as delivery arrangements, are not direct marketing, but that a message with significant promotional material is, and then the marketing rules apply ([ICO guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/)). In Germany, the Hamburg Higher Regional Court ruled in March 2026 that even a newsletter hint inside an order confirmation counts as advertising without consent ([summary](https://shopbetreiber-blog.de/olg-hamburg-werbung-in-der-bestellbestaetigung-ohne-einwilligung-unzulaessig)). Keep the message plain: "Your book is in. We're open until 6." In the US, automated texts fall under the federal TCPA, so ask for permission to text when you take the number.

**Saying it's an AI.** For customers in the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act has required since 2 August 2026 that people are told when they interact with an AI system, as the [Commission's FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) explains. Even where the law doesn't ask for it, say it in the first sentence.

**Distance contracts.** Under the [UK Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134), a contract concluded entirely by phone, chat or form can be a distance contract, and consumers can usually cancel those within 14 days. One confirmed at the counter is not. Decide which you're doing, word your order confirmation to match, and ask a trade body or a solicitor if you're unsure.

## A two-week pilot

I'd run the assistant on last month's slips first and count three things: correct matches, near misses, and wrong titles. Then go live with a bookseller approving every title for the first month. One wrong book ordered is a reason to tighten the rules, not a number to average away.

## Questions booksellers ask

### Can AI identify a book from a vague description?

Often, when it searches a real catalogue and the customer remembers a few details: the topic, where they heard of it, roughly when. Cover colour is a weak clue, and memory of a title is often wrong. Treat a match as a suggestion for the bookseller to confirm, never as an answer.

### Will customers prefer a bot to a bookseller?

Most don't have to choose. The assistant answers when nobody can, in the evening or at the busy Saturday till, and a person takes over whenever the customer asks. The goal is that no request waits on a slip.

### Does this help against online retailers?

It closes the gap on speed and convenience: a clear reply within minutes and a book next morning. It won't change price competition where prices aren't fixed, so in the UK and US it works best as a reason to choose you rather than a reason to discount.

### What if the wrong book gets ordered?

That's why the customer sees the cover and title before anything is ordered. Decide your policy for uncollected orders in advance: how long you hold them, and whether you ask for a deposit on expensive or unusual titles.

## Where I'd start

Start at the end of the process, not the clever part. Make the pickup message automatic first, which needs no AI. Then add the vague-request assistant for evenings and busy Saturdays, with a bookseller approving every title. The assistant finds, the bookseller decides, and the customer always hears when the book is in.
