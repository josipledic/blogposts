A freelance designer who wants two quiet days a week and a founder whose team of four will be eight by spring both click the same "Book a tour" button. They get the same price list, the same tour and the same "let me know if you have questions". One of them ends up on the wrong plan and leaves after three months. The other signs somewhere else, because a competitor sent a proposal the same afternoon.

Coworking spaces don't lose members because the space is bad. They lose them in the gaps between the first click and the signed agreement, and again when people end up on a plan that doesn't fit how they work. Most of those gaps are about information: nobody asked the right questions early enough, and nobody wrote down the answers.

Here is a teardown of how a typical small space sells today, step by step, and what changes when a model handles the asking, matching and follow-up while the community manager does the part that sells: the tour.

Margins in coworking are thin and occupancy decides them. With a third of desks empty on average, every inquiry that goes cold, and every member who leaves because they were on the wrong plan, shows up directly in the numbers.

## Teardown: how a tour request goes today

Picture a space with 120 desks, a handful of private offices and two meeting rooms, run by the owner and one community manager. Here's what happens to a typical inquiry.

### Step 1: The form asks almost nothing

Name, email, phone, "message". Most people write "I'd like to book a tour" and nothing else. The space now knows nothing about how this person works, what they need, or when they want to start.

**What's lost:** everything that would make the reply and the tour relevant.

### Step 2: The reply comes tomorrow, with a PDF

The community manager answers the next morning between member requests and a broken printer. The reply is friendly and generic: a price list with seven plans and "when would you like to come by?"

**What's lost:** a day, and the prospect's patience. Seven plans with no guidance means the person has to work out alone which one fits, which most people won't.

### Step 3: Three emails to find a time

Tuesday? No. Thursday afternoon? Only after four. Friday at ten? Fine. By the time the tour is set, the prospect has seen two other spaces.

**What's lost:** momentum. Scheduling by email is the easiest step to fix and the one most often left alone.

### Step 4: The tour is the same for everyone

The community manager walks the prospect through the whole space: café, hot desks, fixed desks, offices, meeting rooms, phone booths, roof terrace. Only halfway through does it come out that this person has five client calls a day and needs a business address for their company registration.

**What's lost:** focus. The tour spent ten minutes on the roof terrace and two on the phone booths, which were the deciding factor.

### Step 5: "Let me know if you have questions"

The prospect leaves with a brochure. There's no written proposal and no date to decide by. The follow-up exists only in the community manager's head, which is full.

**What's lost:** the decision. People who don't get a clear proposal tend to postpone, and postponing often means signing somewhere else.

### Step 6: The wrong plan, if they sign

If they do sign, they often pick the cheapest hot desk plan from the list, then discover that the quiet corner is always taken and phone booths are booked out by ten. Three months later they cancel.

**What's lost:** the member, after all the effort to win them.

## The rebuilt process

The fixes aren't complicated. Ask the right questions at the start, recommend one or two plans with reasons, make booking a tour a click, brief the tour host, send a proposal the same day, and follow up on a schedule. A model can do the asking, drafting and remembering. People give the tour and make the offer.

## Matching people to plans

The heart of the process is the match. Most spaces have a handful of membership types, and the right one follows from a few facts about how someone works. The model learns your plans and suggests the fit; the community manager checks it.

| What they tell you | Likely fit | Show first on the tour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One or two days a week, flexible schedule | Flex plan or day pass bundle | Hot desk area, lockers, café |
| Four or five days, same setup every day, second monitor | Fixed desk | Fixed desk zone, storage, quiet areas |
| Many calls, confidential work | Private office, or fixed desk with phone booth credits | Phone booths, soundproofing, meeting rooms |
| Team of three to six, growing | Team office with expansion option | Offices, expansion path, contract terms |
| Needs an address, rarely on site | Virtual office or business address | Mail handling, meeting room credits |
| Works evenings or weekends | Plan with 24/7 access | Access system, security, lighting |
| Runs workshops or client sessions | Membership plus meeting room package | Meeting rooms, event space, equipment |

The questions behind the table are short and practical, so the inquiry doesn't feel like a form: "How many days a week do you expect to be here? Do you have many calls? Are you coming alone or with a team? Anything you definitely need, like a business address, parking or 24/7 access?"

## What the tour host receives

Instead of a name and an email, the person giving the tour gets a brief like this:

> **Thursday 10:00, Maria Keller, freelance UX designer.** Three days a week, usually Tuesday to Thursday. Two or three client video calls a day, needs a quiet spot for concentrated work. Cycles in. Wants to start 1 November. Currently works from home and a café. **Suggested:** flex plan (12 days) plus phone booth credits, or a fixed desk if she wants the same spot. **Show first:** quiet zone, phone booths, bike room. **Ask about:** whether she'd use the address for her business.

The tour now starts with what matters to Maria, and the community manager already knows what to recommend. It takes the model a few seconds to write; it saves the first ten minutes of every tour.

## The honest recommendation sells better

It's tempting to set up an assistant that always nudges people toward the bigger plan. Don't. The best recommendation is the smallest plan that fits how someone actually works, with a clear upgrade path. People on the right plan stay longer and upgrade on their own when their needs grow. People pushed onto the wrong plan leave, and tell others why.

## The same-day proposal

After the tour, the model drafts a proposal from the brief and the community manager's notes, and the manager sends it, usually with a line or two added in their own words:

> Hi Maria, thanks for coming by today. Based on what you told us, three days a week with regular client calls, we'd suggest the Flex 12 plan with 10 hours of phone booth time a month, €235 per month, starting 1 November. If you'd rather have the same desk every time, a fixed desk is €340. We can hold the November start for you until next Friday. If you'd like to try a full day first, just reply with a date. Best, Tom

Prices and availability come from your management software, never from the model's memory. If the plan list or pricing changes, the proposals change with it.

## Day passes are tours too

Many people try a space with a day pass before they talk to anyone. They're prospects who already walked in. A short message after the visit, asking how the day went and suggesting the plan that fits what they did (booked a phone booth twice, stayed until seven), turns more of those visits into conversations. Some coworking software vendors recommend following up within 48 hours of a first visit, while the day is still fresh.

## Where people stay in charge

- **Discounts and special terms.** The model can say that longer commitments cost less if that's your policy. Negotiated deals belong to the manager.
- **Corporate and broker inquiries.** Companies looking for team space, and broker platforms that send leads, need a person early, with the full picture.
- **Community.** Introducing new members to each other, noticing who's struggling, deciding who fits: that's the work a model can't do and shouldn't pretend to.
- **Contracts.** Terms, notice periods and deposits come from your standard agreement, not from a chatbot's summary.

And if your space has 25 desks and gets five inquiries a month, you don't need a model. A better form with four questions, a booking link for tours and a proposal template will get you most of the benefit.

## Tools that fit

Coworking management platforms such as Nexudus, OfficeRnD, Optix, Cobot and Spacebring handle memberships, bookings, billing and often leads and tours. Many connect to CRMs like HubSpot, and several have added AI features. The model described here sits on top: a guided inquiry that asks the right questions, a plan match from your live price list, a tour brief, a proposal draft and a follow-up schedule.

Prospect data is personal data. Keep it in your CRM or management platform, delete it after a reasonable time if people don't sign, and tell visitors when they're chatting with an assistant.

## Questions operators ask

### Should we publish prices on the website?

Usually yes, at least "from" prices. People who can't find prices often don't ask; they leave. The guided inquiry then refines the number instead of revealing it.

### Can the model give virtual tours?

It can send a video tour or photos of the areas that matter to this person, which helps with people relocating from another city. The real tour, with a person, still decides most memberships.

### Won't a guided inquiry put people off?

Keep it short and optional. Four or five questions, clearly useful, with a way to just book a tour without answering. Most people answer because they'd rather not sit through a tour of things they don't need.

### How do we measure whether it works?

Track time to first reply, the share of inquiries that book a tour, the share of tours that get a proposal the same day, tour-to-member conversion, and how many new members change or cancel their plan in the first three months.

## Rule of thumb

Ask how people work before they visit, recommend the smallest plan that fits, brief whoever gives the tour, and send a clear proposal the same day. Then follow up twice, politely, and stop.
