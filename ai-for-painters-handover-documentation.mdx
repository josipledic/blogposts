Picture a hallway repaint that finished three weeks ago. The invoice is paid. Then an email arrives from the customer: a photo of a hairline crack above the front door and a faint yellow patch near the ceiling, with the sentence "This wasn't there before you came."

The painter thinks it was. He took photos on day one, he is fairly sure. They are somewhere among 140 pictures from that month: other sites, a ladder, a colour card, his daughter's football match, all named IMG followed by a number. Finding the right three takes an hour of scrolling, and the wide shot that would show the crack in context was never taken.

Nothing in this story is about paint quality. It is about proof, and proof is mostly a filing problem. That is a job AI is good at, as long as a person still decides what the photos mean.

## Why the argument is really about the moment of acceptance

In German contract law, which I will use as the main example, one date splits the risk: the formal acceptance, the Abnahme. Before it, the contractor has to show the work is free of defects. After it, the customer has to show a defect was already there. Legal commentary calls this the reversal of the burden of proof ([Kanzlei Kübler](https://kanzlei-kuebler.biz/mangel-nach-abnahme-beweislast/)). Two exceptions matter: defects that were fraudulently concealed, and defects listed in the acceptance protocol, which stay the contractor's problem until they are fixed and re-inspected.

| | Before acceptance | After acceptance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who proves what | Painter shows the work is defect-free | Customer shows the defect existed at handover |
| Final payment | Not yet due | Due |
| Warranty clock | Not running | Starts |
| What photos are for | Showing the condition you handed over | Answering "was this already there?" |

The acceptance rules are in [section 640 of the German Civil Code (BGB)](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__640.html). A customer cannot refuse acceptance over minor defects. If a customer knowingly accepts defective work, they lose several of their rights unless they reserve them at acceptance. And a work is deemed accepted if the contractor sets a reasonable deadline for acceptance after completion and the customer does not refuse within it by naming at least one defect. For consumers, the contractor must also have pointed out these consequences in text form when setting the deadline. The VOB/B construction terms, where agreed, use 12 working days after a request and six working days after the customer starts using the work ([VOB/B section 12](https://dejure.org/gesetze/VOB-B/12.html)). Private customers often never agree to the VOB/B, so check your own contracts. This is the state of things as I checked it in September 2026, and it is not legal advice.

The practical point: your photos matter most on two dates, the day before you start and the day you hand over.

There are about 38,000 painting and varnishing businesses in Germany, 37,954 at the end of June 2025 according to the [Bundesverband Farbe Gestaltung Bautenschutz](https://www.farbe.de/fileadmin/Bundesverband_Farbe/Bundesverband/Mitarbeiter/Naser/Zahlen_Daten_Fakten_zum_Maler_und_Lackiererhandwerk_2026.pdf). Most are small. Almost none of them has a document controller.

## How the photos go missing

Most painting businesses I have looked at fall into the same three habits.

**The camera roll.** Everything goes into the private phone. Nothing is labelled, and customer photos sit next to family photos.

**The messaging thread.** Photos travel through WhatsApp to the office. They are compressed, unsorted, and gone when someone changes phone or leaves the company.

**The protocol on paper.** A signed Abnahmeprotokoll exists for big jobs, but its defect list says "minor touch-ups" and nothing more, with no pictures attached.

## Four moments that need a photo

The routine is short. The discipline is doing it on every job, including the small ones, because small jobs are where nobody writes anything down.

<Timeline title="The photo routine around one job">
<Phase when="Before the first brush" title="Condition on arrival">One wide shot per room, plus close-ups of anything already damaged: cracks, stains, chipped frames, scratched floors, loose sockets. Speak a short note while you shoot.</Phase>
<Phase when="During the work" title="What will be hidden">Photograph what the final coat covers: patched cracks, filler, primer on stained areas, the product labels and batch numbers. If you had to tell the customer about a substrate problem, photograph that too.</Phase>
<Phase when="At completion" title="Walk-through with the customer">Wide and close shot of every room in daylight and with the lights the customer normally uses. Any item the customer points out goes into the protocol with a photo, in their presence.</Phase>
<Phase when="Within a week" title="Protocol and archive">The signed protocol and the photos are stored together under the job, not on anyone's private phone. Keep them for as long as claims are possible.</Phase>
</Timeline>

A note on the first moment. Photographing existing damage before you start does two things. It protects you from claims for damage you did not cause, and it is also a polite way to tell the customer what you found. "That crack was there when I arrived, do you want me to fill it as an extra?" turns a future dispute into a quote.

Taking the photos is the human part. Everything after that is admin, and the admin is where AI saves time.

The voice notes do most of the work. Standing in a hallway, saying "hallway, north wall, hairline crack above the door frame, about 40 centimetres, already there" takes ten seconds. The model attaches that sentence to the right photo. Nobody types on a phone with paint on their fingers.

It is the same pattern as [turning voice notes into construction daily reports](/posts/ai-for-construction-daily-reports): spoken facts on site, structured text in the office, a person who checks it before it leaves the building.

## Protocol wording: specific beats vague

AI is especially useful for one boring thing: wording open points precisely. Compare two entries.

```text
Vague:  Painting partly defective, to be touched up.

Specific:  Living room, wall to the kitchen: paint runs on about 2 m2,
           left of the light switch (photo 14). Repaint by 30 October.
           Customer reserves rights for this item.
```

The second version names a place, a size, a photo and a deadline. It also matters legally. Under section 640 paragraph 3 of the BGB, a customer who accepts work with a known defect loses certain rights unless they reserve them at acceptance, and a blanket reservation is unlikely to do that job. Specific entries protect both sides: the customer keeps their rights for the listed item, and you can see exactly what you owe and what you do not.

If an item belongs in the protocol but you do not think it is a defect, write it down anyway, along with your view. "Slight roller texture visible in raking light, customer notes it, contractor considers it within tolerance" is an honest entry. The customer can sign it, or disagree in writing.

## The raking light problem

A large share of disputes between painters and customers are about looks, not function. A wall that looks flat under a ceiling light shows every roller mark when the low afternoon sun hits it. That is why expert guidance exists. The Bundesverband Farbe has published a [guide for classifying optical defects for expert witnesses](https://www.farbe.de/aktuell/nachricht/artikel/leitfaden-zur-einordnung-des-optischen-mangels-fuer-sachverstaendige/), which deals with grazing light as an assessment condition. And the industry's quality levels Q1 to Q4 for drywall and plaster surfaces are set out in [BFS-Information 23-01](https://www.farbe-bfs.de/aktuelles/news/bfs-information-23-01/). The standard level, Q2, is not a promise that marks cannot appear in raking light.

<Callout title="Write the surface quality into the quote" tone="warning">
If the customer expects a wall without any visible mark under any light, that is a higher surface level than standard, and it costs more work. Name the level in the quote and in the protocol, and photograph the finished wall in normal light and, if the customer asks, with a torch held at a low angle. An AI assistant must not declare a surface acceptable or defective. That call belongs to you, and in a real dispute, to an expert.
</Callout>

## Data protection for photos taken in people's homes

A room photo can contain more than paint. Family photos on a shelf, post on the table, a screen, a child. Such photos are personal data, and in practice the contract itself is usually cited as the legal basis for documentation photos under Article 6(1)(b) GDPR, as [practical guidance for tradespeople](https://123erfasst.de/rechtlich-abgesichert-mit-fotodokumentation/) describes. That covers documentation of the work. It does not cover using the same photos as marketing references, which needs the customer's written consent.

My suggestions:

- Frame photos on walls, ceilings and surfaces. Move or avoid people and personal items.
- Store job photos in a company system or app with a processing agreement, not in private messaging apps.
- If an AI service reads captions and photos, check where it processes data, sign a data processing agreement, and confirm the provider does not train on your data.
- Decide on retention. The length of the warranty period is the natural reference: two years for ordinary work, five for work on a building that counts as a structural work under [section 634a of the BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__634a.html). Case law applies the five-year period to painting only when it is extensive and significant for the building, such as a complete renovation, as [this overview of painters' warranty periods](https://baurechtsuche.de/mandantenfragen/gewaehrleistung-wie-lange-muessen-wir-als-maler-fuer-unsere-malerarbeiten-haften/) explains. When in doubt, keep the job file for five years, then delete.

## A note for England and Wales

The wording differs, the idea does not. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, a trader must perform a service with reasonable care and skill, and a consumer can ask for repeat performance or a price reduction ([explanatory notes to section 49](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/49/notes)). What is expected depends on the trade, industry standards and the price. A written snagging list at handover, with location, description and photo for each item, is the closest thing to an Abnahmeprotokoll ([Designing Buildings on snagging](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Snagging_construction_works)), and the same photo routine serves both systems.

## What it takes to set up

The cost is modest, and the work is in the habit.

- **A shared place for job photos.** A construction photo app or a folder structure with one folder per job. Trade software such as Craftboxx, Craftnote or Plancraft in Germany, or CompanyCam in English-speaking markets, has this built in. If you already use one of them, start there.
- **A voice note and photo pipeline.** A transcription model plus a language model that matches notes to photos and writes captions. This is a small piece of integration work, typically a few days for someone like me, and then a few cents per job in running cost. Both figures are rough estimates, not quotes.
- **A protocol template.** One page, with the open points table. Your own wording, your quality levels, your reservation text, reviewed once by a lawyer or your guild.

I would pilot it on one or two crews for a month and count how often the handover report is ready the same day.

## When you do not need AI

If you do four jobs a month, a folder per job and a checklist on the back of the work order does the job. AI starts to pay once several crews shoot photos on many sites at once, and nobody in the office has the time to sort, caption and compile them.

## Questions painters ask

### Are phone photos worth anything in a dispute?

They help, especially with timestamps and a clear link to the job and room. But they are supporting evidence. A protocol that both sides signed carries more weight than photos alone, and in the end a court or an expert decides. The photos work best as attachments to the protocol.

### What if the customer refuses to sign the protocol?

Write down that you offered, who was present and what was found, and keep the photos. Then set a reasonable written deadline for acceptance. Under section 640(2) of the BGB, acceptance can be deemed to have happened if the customer does not refuse within that deadline by naming at least one defect, and for consumers the notice must explain this consequence in text form. Talk to a lawyer before you rely on it.

### Can AI find defects in my photos?

It can describe what is visible, but it cannot tell you whether something is a defect, since that depends on the contract, the surface quality agreed, the lighting and the substrate. Use it for sorting, captions and checking that the set is complete, and keep the judgement with yourself.

### Do I need the customer's permission to photograph?

For documenting your own work in the contract, the contract is generally enough of a basis, and it helps to say so in your terms and to tell customers at the start. For anything on your website or social media, ask for written consent.

## My rule of thumb

Photograph the room before you touch it, photograph what the paint will hide, and photograph the finished room with the customer standing next to you. Then let the software sort and write it up, and keep the decisions, and the signature, with people.
