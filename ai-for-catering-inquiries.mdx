Friday, 9 p.m. An office manager finally gets round to the task she's been putting off all week: lunch for 60 people at the company's anniversary event in five weeks. She finds four caterers online and sends each of them the same short message through their website forms. Then she goes home.

One caterer replies at 7:30 on Saturday morning with a friendly message, three menu options with a price range per person, a note that the date is still free, and two questions about the venue. The other three reply on Monday and Tuesday. By then, the office manager has already forwarded the first proposal to her boss, with the comment "looks good".

This happens constantly, and the caterers who lose are rarely worse. They're busy. On a Friday night a small caterer is usually doing exactly what they're good at: cooking and serving at someone else's event. The inquiry waits until there's time, and by then someone else has answered.

The HBR study is about sales leads in general and it's from 2011, but anyone who has booked a caterer knows it hasn't got less true. Clients compare several caterers at once, and the first complete, credible reply sets the reference point. Everyone who answers later is compared against it.

The catch is the word "complete". A fast auto-reply saying "thanks, we'll be in touch" doesn't count. What wins is a fast reply that's actually useful.

## What slows the reply down

It's not laziness. A good catering reply needs information and a bit of calculation:

| What the caterer needs | Why it matters | Usually missing from the first message? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and time | Availability of kitchen and staff | Often given |
| Number of guests | Quantities, staff, price | Often given, often vague ("about 50 to 70") |
| Type of event and service style | Buffet, plated, finger food, food truck, drop-off | Often missing |
| Venue and facilities | Kitchen access, power, setup time, distance | Almost always missing |
| Dietary requirements | Vegetarian, vegan, allergies, halal, kosher | Usually missing until later |
| Budget | Which menu level to propose | Usually missing, sometimes on purpose |
| Extras | Drinks, staff, equipment, tableware, cake | Rarely mentioned |

The owner reads the message between courses, thinks "I need to ask about the venue and do a price", and puts it off until Monday. That's where AI fits: it can read the inquiry, check what's known, work out a price range from your own rules, and draft a reply that's both fast and useful.

## The workflow

<Flow title="From website inquiry to a first proposal within the hour" caption="The model reads, checks and drafts from your menus and price rules. The owner approves with a tap. Anything unusual waits for a proper look.">
<Step title="Inquiry arrives" who="Client" time="any time">Website form, email, phone message or a booking platform.</Step>
<Step title="Extract and check" who="AI" tone="ai" time="seconds">Date, headcount, event type, location, any dietary notes. Checks the calendar for the date and the team's capacity that day.</Step>
<Step title="Choose options" who="AI" tone="ai">Picks two or three suitable packages from your menus, based on event type, headcount and any budget hint.</Step>
<Step title="Price range" who="AI" tone="ai">Calculates a range per person from your price rules: menu level, minimums, staff hours, delivery zone.</Step>
<Step title="Owner approves" who="Owner" tone="human" time="1 to 2 min">Reads the draft on the phone, adjusts or approves. Unusual requests get a proper reply later, with a quick holding message now.</Step>
<Step title="Send and follow up" who="AI" tone="ai">Reply goes out. If there's no answer after two days, a short follow-up. After a week, a last friendly check.</Step>
</Flow>

The approval step is short on purpose. The owner isn't writing, just checking: is the date really free, do the menus make sense, is the price range right? For standard packages at standard sizes, some caterers let the model send directly and review afterwards. I'd start with approval for everything and loosen it only once the drafts are consistently right.

That's the message the office manager forwards to her boss. It answers the question she was really asking ("can you do it, roughly how much?") and gives her something easy to reply to.

## Pricing rules the model can use

The price range is where most caterers hesitate to let software help. It works if you write your rules down once:

- **Price per person per package,** with a range to cover variations.
- **Minimum order** or minimum spend per event.
- **Staff:** hours included, rate for extra hours, number of staff per guest count.
- **Delivery zones and fees.**
- **Surcharges:** weekends, public holidays, very short notice.
- **What's always extra:** drinks, equipment hire, cake, cleaning.

With those rules, the model's range is a calculation, not a guess. The owner still approves, and the exact quote follows once the questions are answered.

## Dietary needs and allergies

Catering clients increasingly ask for vegan, gluten-free and allergy-safe options, and a small kitchen can't always guarantee them. The model should collect the numbers ("4 vegetarian, 2 vegan, 1 nut allergy"), pass on what your kitchen can offer from your written information, and route anything about severe allergies to a person. It should never promise "allergen-free" on your behalf. Cross-contamination in a busy catering kitchen is real, and so is the liability.

## Follow-up is half the win

Many catering leads aren't lost to competitors. They're lost to silence. The client got a quote, got busy, and nobody followed up. A short, friendly message after two days ("Just checking whether the proposal fits, or whether we should adjust anything?") recovers a surprising share of them. The model can send it on schedule and stop as soon as the client replies or books.

Keep a simple list of every inquiry with three columns: when it arrived, when you first replied, and whether it became a booking. ## Tools that fit

Catering and event software such as Caterease, Total Party Planner or HoneyBook handles proposals, contracts and deposits. Many small caterers run on email, a calendar and a spreadsheet, and that's fine. The AI layer reads incoming inquiries, checks the calendar, drafts the reply from your menus and price rules, and sends it after approval. It can log every inquiry in a simple list, so you finally see how many you get, how fast you reply and how many you win.

## Questions caterers ask

### Should the first reply include prices?

A range, yes. Clients comparing caterers want to know if you're in their budget before investing more time. A range per person with clear inclusions is honest and useful; the exact quote follows once you know the details.

### What about weddings, which are much more personal?

The first reply works the same way: availability, a few options, a range, a couple of questions, and an offer to meet or call. The personal part starts with the tasting and the conversation, which stay entirely with you. Speed simply gets you to that conversation.

### Will clients notice the reply is AI-drafted?

Not if it's specific to their inquiry and you've approved it. Let the model write in your voice using your past replies as examples, and sign with a real name. In the EU, if a chatbot answers clients directly without review, it has to say it's automated.

### What about inquiries that come in by phone?

If you miss the call, a voicemail transcription or a short form link by text message turns it into the same written inquiry, and the same workflow takes over. If you take the call, jot down the key facts while you talk and let the model turn your notes into the written proposal afterwards. Either way, the client gets something in writing the same day, which is what they'll forward to whoever decides.

### What if the date is already booked?

The model says so immediately and offers alternatives: a nearby date, a smaller format you can still fit in, or a colleague you recommend. A quick, helpful "no" is remembered too.

## Rule of thumb

Reply within the hour, with something the client can use: availability, a few fitting options, an honest price range and one or two good questions. Let the model draft it from your own menus and rules, and keep your own time for cooking and for the conversations that close the booking.
