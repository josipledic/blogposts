It's the first warm Saturday in April, and the phone at a mid-sized garden centre hasn't stopped since nine. One caller wants to know if the 5-litre hydrangeas are in. Another asks whether lavender will survive in a pot on a north-facing balcony. Someone sends a photo of a rose with brown spots and the message "what is this?". A fourth caller asks whether you close at five or six. A fifth wants to know if a lily is safe around her cat.

Five questions, five different jobs. The first and fourth need only a stock screen and a sheet of opening hours. The second takes some plant knowledge. The third needs someone who can look at the leaf. The fifth should never be answered from memory by anybody.

That is the problem with putting an AI assistant on a garden centre's phone or chat. It all looks like "plant questions", and an assistant that treats them as one category will be right most of the time and dangerously wrong some of the time. So here is my ranking of the question types, with a verdict on each.

A third of the year's turnover arrives in three months, and as the UK figure shows, the peak follows the weather, not the calendar. When the sun comes out, questions arrive in a wave nobody can roster for. Today they land on whoever is closest to the phone, usually someone in the middle of serving a customer.

The third number matters for a different reason. Supermarkets and discounters sell plants too, but they don't answer "will this survive on my balcony?". Advice is hard to copy. So automate the routine questions to free your plant people for the ones only they can answer.

## The ranking, from most asked to least

I ordered these by how often I'd expect each type in a spring week. The numbers are assumed, not measured.

### 1. Hours, directions and services

Automate fully. Opening hours (including bank holidays), parking, dogs, click and collect, Christmas tree preorders. The answer lives in one fact sheet that someone owns. The usual failure isn't the AI, it's the fact sheet going stale after Easter.

### 2. "Do you have it?" Stock and price

Automate when the stock data is current. An assistant connected to your till can say "yes, 12 of the 5-litre hydrangeas at 9:15 this morning". If stock is updated by hand once a week, it should say "usually in stock, call or reserve online" and nothing more. A confident wrong "yes" sends someone on a 30-minute drive. Plants share the shelf-life problem of [farm shop produce](/posts/ai-for-farm-shops-inventory): system and bench drift apart quickly.

### 3. Planting and care basics

Automate, but from your own care cards. When to plant, how deep, sun or shade, watering, feeding, pot size. A short card for each of your top 100 sellers, written or approved by your head grower, is the source. The assistant quotes the card and doesn't improvise. A model answering from general memory gives average advice, which can be wrong for your variety.

### 4. Hardiness and "the right plant for my spot"

Answer from product data, after asking where the customer lives and which way the spot faces. Hardiness labels are the trap. The RHS rates plants H1a to H7 by [the lowest temperature they are likely to withstand](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/trials-awards/award-of-garden-merit/rhs-hardiness-rating), with H4 meaning roughly minus 10 to minus 5 degrees Celsius. It notes that these are absolute minimum winter temperatures, not the long-term average annual extreme minimum that [USDA zones use](https://planthardiness.ars.usda.gov/). Labels from two systems on one shelf are not interchangeable, and a model that mixes them up produces confident nonsense. The assistant quotes the label on your product record and adds the customer's location. Borderline cases go to a person.

### 5. Large plants, delivery and collection

Collect, then hand over. A 2-metre tree with a root ball is a logistics job: weight, garden access, van or crane, date, price. The assistant gathers the address, photos of the access and the preferred day for whoever schedules deliveries. A person confirms slot and price.

### 6. Returns, guarantees and "it died"

Explain the policy, collect the evidence, let a person decide. In Germany, statutory warranty lasts two years, and in the first 12 months the law presumes the defect existed at purchase, [say the consumer advice centres](https://www.verbraucherzentrale.de/wissen/vertraege-reklamation/kundenrechte/alles-zu-gewaehrleistung-und-garantie-5057). In the UK the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives a [30-day short-term right to reject](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/22) faulty goods, and for [six months](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds) the seller must prove the goods weren't faulty when sold. With plants the hard question is cause: an [insurer's legal portal](https://www.ergo.de/de/rechtsportal/rechtsfrage-des-tages/gewaehrleistung-beim-pflanzenkauf) notes that a shrub nobody watered is no defect, while a sick plant sold as healthy can be. The assistant shouldn't decide that. It asks for the receipt, photos of the plant and its position, and how it was watered, then passes the file on.

### 7. Pests and diseases, usually with a photo

Collect, then hand over. The temptation is biggest here, because a photo feels like the perfect input for AI.

<Callout title="A photo is not a diagnosis" tone="warning">
Plant identification apps can do reasonably well on wild species, but results vary a lot. A 2023 field test of six apps on native Irish plants found the best one named the species on the first try for 88% of flowers and 80% of leaves, and even that one was wrong between 12% and 23% of the time. Disease diagnosis is harder: a widely cited 2016 study saw a model that scored about 99% on lab photos fall to about 31% on photos taken under different conditions. A customer's phone photo, blurry leaf and fence behind, is exactly that. Let the assistant ask for better photos and context. Let a person name the problem.
</Callout>

Sources: the [PLOS ONE app comparison](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10075466/) and the [2016 Frontiers study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.01419/full). Models have improved since, but a [recent review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13066816/) still calls the gap between tidy test photos and real ones a major limitation. The app test used wild plants, not cultivars. I'd expect named varieties to be harder, but that is my reasoning, not a result.

The assistant can triage well: ask for a leaf close-up, the whole plant, the position and when symptoms started, then write it up for your plant expert.

### 8. "Is it safe for my cat, dog or child?"

Answer only from a list you maintain, and point to the poison centre for anything else. Small volume, large consequence. [International Cat Care](https://icatcare.org/articles/lily-poisoning-in-cats), in an article reviewed by the UK's Veterinary Poisons Information Service, warns that any part of a lily, including pollen licked off fur, can cause kidney damage and death in cats, and that "pollen-free" lilies are no safe alternative. The German poison centre GIZ-Nord [counted about 5,100 plant-related inquiries in 2025](https://herzogtum-direkt.de/index.php/2026/05/23/achtung-vor-gartenpflanzen/) out of nearly 60,000, according to its medical director as quoted in a health insurer's press release. If a plant is on your reviewed toxicity list, the assistant quotes it. If not, it never says "safe". It says it doesn't know and gives the local poison information number.

### 9. Weedkillers and plant protection products

Always a person. Here a regulation, not just prudence, draws the line. In the EU, [Article 6 of Directive 2009/128/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2009/128/2019-07-26/eng) requires distributors to have trained staff available at the time of sale and to give buyers of non-professional products general information on risks. In Germany, [the BVL states](https://www.bvl.bund.de/DE/Arbeitsbereiche/04_Pflanzenschutzmittel/05_Haendler/03_VorschriftenAbgabePSM/psm_VorschriftenAbgabePSM_node.html) that these products may not be sold in self-service and that merely pointing to the instructions is not enough, and the [Brandenburg plant protection service](https://www.isip.de/brandenburg/pflanzenschutzdienst/pflanzenschutz-kontrollwesen-risikomanangement/handel-mit-pflanzenschutzmitteln/grundsaetzliches-zum-handel-mit-pflanzenschutzmitteln-390942) adds that sellers need expertise (Sachkunde) even for hobby gardeners. The UK has its own rules since Brexit, so check yours with the HSE or your trade body. Either way, a chatbot shouldn't recommend a chemical. It can say "that needs our qualified adviser" and book a callback.

New question types will appear. Sort them by how often they come in and by what happens if the answer is wrong.

## What it takes, and what it costs

The effort is mostly yours. The assistant needs a fact sheet with hours, services and policies, a care card for each best seller, and a stock feed from your till or shop system that is no more than a day old. With those, the assistant is a small project. Without them, writing them is the project, and it pays off with or without AI.

My rough estimate, not a quote: a few days of staff time for the fact sheet and the first 50 to 100 care cards, a few days of setup, and running costs in the tens to low hundreds of euros a month at the volume of one garden centre. Check current vendor prices.

The time saved is smaller than people expect. Assumptions: 200 questions a spring week, 64% routine, three minutes of staff time each including the interruption. That is about 6 hours a week, or 96 hours over a 15-week spring. At an assumed 20 euros an hour, about 1,900 euros. Real, not dramatic. The bigger value is timing: the call at ten on a sunny Saturday gets answered while every pair of hands is on the floor.

## When a simpler fix wins

Check these first. A correct Google Business Profile with holiday hours cuts "are you open?" more than any chatbot. A stock tick on the website ("in stock", "low", "ask in store") removes much of rank 2. A care card with a QR code at the bench answers rank 3 at the plant. If three quarters of your tally is hours and stock, you need those three things, not an assistant.

## Data protection and disclosure

Customer photos can show faces, house numbers and location data, and chat logs hold names and delivery addresses. Keep them in a system you control, set a retention period, and sign a data processing agreement with the provider, ideally with EU hosting.

You also have to say it's an AI. [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) has applied since 2 August 2026 and requires that people are told they are interacting with an AI system, unless that is obvious. I checked the Commission's FAQ in September 2026 and the date stands. One line does it: "I'm the garden centre's automated assistant. A member of staff can take over at any time."

## What garden centre owners ask before they start

### Can an AI chatbot identify a plant from a photo?

Sometimes, and reasonably well for common wild species. For garden varieties, damaged leaves and bad light it's much less reliable. Treat it as a suggestion a person confirms, never as the basis for a diagnosis or product recommendation.

### Can a chatbot recommend weedkiller or pesticides in a garden centre?

I wouldn't allow it. In the EU, selling these products comes with training and information duties, and Germany bans self-service. A chatbot can't meet those, but it can hand the customer to your qualified adviser.

### How do I stop the assistant making up plant advice?

Restrict it to your own sources: fact sheet, stock data and care cards. It quotes them, and when nothing matches it says "I'll pass this to our plant expert". Read a sample of conversations every week for the first month.

## Where I'd start

Count a week of questions first: a tally sheet by the phone, a skim of the inbox. You'll learn which of these nine types dominate your spring, which beats any ranking I can offer.

Then start with the questions that fit on a label: hours, stock and care cards. My rule of thumb: if an answer could sit on a card next to the plant, the assistant can say it. If someone has to look at the plant, read a regulation or take responsibility, a person answers, and the assistant gets that person a complete case.
