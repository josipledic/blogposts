AI can help a small asbestos surveying firm keep every client's register alive after the survey: each material entered once with its score and photo, each re-inspection booked before it is due, and each contractor told what is in the wall before they drill it. The survey itself, the sampling and the lab result stay exactly where they are. What usually breaks is everything after the PDF goes out: the register in a spreadsheet the client never opens, the re-inspection nobody booked, the electrician who never saw page 34. A language model can draft those parts from your own report data, while plain rules count down the dates. Your surveyors still decide what a material is and how it scores.

The duty to keep that information current sits with your clients, and it does not end with the survey.

[Regulation 4 of the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/632/regulation/4/made) requires the dutyholder to record the assessment, write a plan that covers monitoring the condition of every known or presumed asbestos-containing material (ACM), and make sure information about its location and condition is "provided to every person liable to disturb it". The plan has to be "reviewed and revised at regular intervals". The duty also covers the common parts of blocks of flats: foyers, corridors, lift shafts, roof spaces and garages ([L143, paragraph 89](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/l143.pdf)).

## Why the follow-up matters

Asbestos still kills thousands of people in Britain every year, decades after its use stopped, and the people most at risk are the ones who worked on buildings.

HSE's summary says that "men who worked in the building industry when asbestos was used extensively in the past continue to be most at risk", and its [asbestos-related disease statistics](https://www.hse.gov.uk/statistics/assets/docs/asbestos-related-disease.pdf) put the yearly total at around 5,000 deaths. Today's exposure happens when a maintenance worker drills, cuts or lifts something nobody told them about. That is a records problem as much as a survey problem.

## Where it breaks today

- **The register is a PDF.** The client gets a 60-page report with a register table at the back. When a tenant changes or a ceiling tile breaks, nobody can find the entry, so nobody updates it.
- **Re-inspections are not booked.** L143 says the plan must be reviewed at least every 12 months and that the frequency of condition checks depends on where the ACM is and how the building is used ([paragraph 144](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/l143.pdf)). In practice the date lives in the surveyor's calendar or nowhere.
- **Re-inspection reports are retyped.** The surveyor walks the building with last year's register on paper, then retypes every entry with a new condition note and score.
- **Contractors are not told.** L143 says that where ACMs are not labelled, the dutyholder must make sure those who might work on them "know that it contains or may contain asbestos, before they start work" ([paragraph 138](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/l143.pdf)). The facilities manager has to extract that from the report, by hand, every time.
- **Refurbishment surveys come too late.** A management survey does not cover intrusive work. HSG264 says a refurbishment and demolition survey "is needed before any refurbishment or demolition work is carried out" ([HSG264, paragraph 51](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg264.pdf)). Clients often find out when the builder is already booked.

## Where AI fits

**Turn the report into a register once.** The model reads your survey report and lab certificate and fills a structured register: location, material, sample reference or "presumed", extent, photo, the four material assessment scores and the total. Plain code checks that every sample reference matches the lab result and that every score adds up. HSG264 scores product type, damage, surface treatment and asbestos type from 1 to 3 each, and treats presumed ACMs as crocidolite unless there is strong evidence otherwise; 10 or more is high potential for fibre release, 7 to 9 medium, 5 to 6 low ([paragraphs 125 to 127](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg264.pdf)). The model never assigns a score the surveyor did not record.

**Count down every re-inspection.** Rules, not the model, set the next date per item from your risk rating and the 12-month plan review, then send the client a booking request a month before. High-scoring items in busy areas come round sooner.

**Pre-fill the re-inspection sheet.** Before the visit, the surveyor gets last year's entries with photos, scores and locations on a tablet. On site they confirm, change or add. The model drafts the condition notes from their dictation and highlights every score that went up.

**Brief the contractor.** When the client logs a job ("replace the boiler flue in plant room 2"), the model pulls every register entry for that room, drafts a one-page briefing with photos and locations, and flags when the job is intrusive enough to need a refurbishment survey first. The facilities manager checks and sends it.

**Draft the annual review.** For each site, the model drafts the plan review: what changed, which items were removed or repaired, which scores rose, what is due next year. The surveyor edits and signs it.

## Where it goes wrong

<Callout title="The model never identifies asbestos" tone="warning">
Whether a material contains asbestos is a lab result or a surveyor's presumption, never a model's guess from a photo. The model copies scores and results from your records, drafts text around them and flags gaps. It never changes a score, removes an entry or tells anyone a material is safe.
</Callout>

**Presumed stays presumed.** An entry without a sample stays "presumed" with the crocidolite score until a sample says otherwise. The register software should make that the default, not a choice.

**The register is the client's record.** Your client remains the dutyholder. Give them read access to their live register and briefings, keep your survey reports as the source and log every change with a name and date.

**Briefings need the right room.** A briefing for the wrong floor is worse than none. Tie every entry to a drawing reference and room number, and send anything ambiguous back to the surveyor.

## Tools that fit

Asbestos management platforms already hold registers, risk scores and re-inspection dates, and several offer site apps for surveyors. If you use one, the gaps are usually turning legacy PDF reports into entries, drafting notes from dictation, contractor briefings by room and the annual review draft. A custom layer pays off when your clients' registers are spread across old reports from several firms, or when facilities managers need briefings in their own job system rather than another login.

## Questions asbestos surveyors ask

### Can AI read asbestos from a photo?

No. A photo can suggest a material looks like a known ACM, but only sampling and lab analysis, or a recorded presumption by a competent surveyor, decide what goes in the register. Use AI for the paperwork around that decision.

### How often should asbestos be re-inspected?

The regulations require monitoring at regular intervals without fixing a number. HSE's approved code of practice says the management plan, including records and drawings, should be reviewed at least every 12 months, and that the frequency of condition checks depends on the location of the ACMs and how the building is used. A sensible default is to check every item at least at the annual review and higher-risk items more often.

### Is this worth it for a two-surveyor firm?

Often, because the same two people survey, write reports and answer clients' "where is the asbestos in room 4?" calls. Start with turning reports into live registers and the re-inspection countdown. Add contractor briefings once clients are using the register.

## A rule for every register

Every material should have one entry with its score and photo, a booked next re-inspection and a briefing ready for anyone who works near it. Let AI do the retyping and drafting, and keep every judgement about what a material is and how dangerous it is with your surveyors.
