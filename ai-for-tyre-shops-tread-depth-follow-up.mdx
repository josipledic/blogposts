AI can help a tyre shop or garage act on the tread readings it already takes. Every service, MOT and puncture repair produces a depth per tyre, and most of those numbers sit in a job card nobody looks at again. The useful setup is simple: plain rules in your system pick out the customers whose tyres are getting close to your threshold, and a language model writes each of them a short message with their own readings, a fitting slot and a quote your team has checked. Replies get sorted, tyres get ordered before the car arrives, and nobody gets a vague "time for new tyres?" mailshot.

The need is easy to see in the government's own MOT figures.

The tyre share has barely moved in years. In the [DVSA tables](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistical-data-sets/mot-testing-data-for-great-britain), tyres show up in between 6.2% and 6.9% of car and van tests every year since 2019-20, and they have made up close to 60% of dangerous defects in every one of those years. Most of these tyres did not wear out overnight. Somebody measured them at an earlier visit.

## What the law and the MOT already give you

The [Highway Code](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/annex-6-vehicle-maintenance-safety-and-security) says cars, light vans and light trailers must have at least 1.6 mm of tread "across the central three-quarters of the breadth of the tread and around the entire circumference." The MOT checks exactly that. When a tyre passes but is getting close, the tester can list it as an advisory. [GOV.UK describes advisories](https://www.gov.uk/getting-an-mot/mot-test-results) as problems "to monitor or fix in the future."

"Monitor" is the weak spot. The driver gets a printout, puts it in the glovebox, and the next person to look at that tyre is often the tester a year later. Your shop has the reading, the customer's contact details and the tyres in stock. What's missing is someone who turns that reading into a message at the right time.

## Where follow-up breaks today

- **Readings stay on the job card.** The technician writes "NSR 2.4 mm, OSR 2.6 mm" and the customer drives off. Unless the front desk sells the tyres there and then, nothing happens next.
- **One message for everyone.** If there is a reminder at all, it's a general "winter is coming, check your tyres" email. It says nothing the customer couldn't read anywhere.
- **No stock ready.** When the customer does call, the size isn't in stock, the fitting moves to next week, and some customers go elsewhere.
- **Replies nobody owns.** "How much for two?", "I sold the car" and "can you do Saturday?" arrive by text, email and phone. Half of them get answered.
- **Fleet and business customers.** A van owner with six vehicles needs one summary across all of them, not six separate reminders.

## Where AI fits

**A due list from your own readings.** Every week the system lists customers whose last recorded depth is under the threshold you set, plus tyres flagged as advisories or close to the age you use as your cut-off. This part is plain logic on your data, not a language model. It should match your shop system exactly, and you decide the thresholds.

**A message with their numbers in it.** For each customer the model drafts a short message from the job card: "When we serviced your Golf in July, the rear tyres measured 2.4 and 2.6 mm. The legal minimum is 1.6 mm. We have your size in stock and can fit two tomorrow afternoon for £X." The price comes from your system, never from the model. A team member reads each batch before it goes out, at least until you trust it.

**Replies sorted and answered.** Each reply is tagged: wants to book, wants a quote for another brand, has done it elsewhere, sold the car, opted out, or "a person should read this". Booking requests come with real free slots from your calendar. "Done elsewhere" and "sold the car" close the record so the customer doesn't get chased again.

**Tyres ordered before the car arrives.** When a fitting is booked, the size, load index and speed rating go on the order list, so the tyres are on the shelf when the car comes in.

**One summary for fleets.** Business customers get one message per period listing every vehicle, its readings and what you recommend, with one quote to approve.

## Where it goes wrong

<Callout title="The model never judges a tyre" tone="warning">
The technician decides whether a tyre is fine, worn or dangerous. The model only repeats the readings and the recommendation that were recorded. It never tells a customer a tyre is "fine for another few months", and any reply that mentions a puncture, a bulge, vibration or a warning light goes to a person the same day.
</Callout>

**Marketing rules.** A message about a reading you took on their car is close to a service message, but a tyre offer is still marketing. The [ICO's guidance on electronic mail marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/) allows the "soft opt-in" only for your own customers who were given a simple way to opt out when you collected their details and in every message after. Record which channel each customer agreed to.

**Stale readings.** A reading from eleven months ago on a car doing 20,000 miles a year says little. Show the date of the reading in the message and in the due list, and put very old readings in a "check at next visit" list instead.

**Prices from the model.** Prices, stock and slots come from your systems. If the model writes a number that isn't in the data it was given, the message should not go out.

## Tools that fit

Start with your garage or tyre shop software. Many can record tread depth per position and send service reminders. If yours already does both well, the missing pieces are usually the message written from the readings and a reply inbox that sorts itself. A custom layer pays off when readings are stored as free text, when replies come in on three channels, or when you run several sites or fleet accounts.

## Questions shop owners ask

### Won't customers feel pressured?

Less than with a generic offer. A message that says "we measured 2.4 mm on your rear tyres in July" is information they can check. Offer one slot and one price, and make it easy to say no.

### Can AI read tread depth from a photo?

There are camera-based tools that measure tread, and they can speed up the measuring. That's a separate decision. The follow-up described here works with whatever readings your technicians already take.

### Is this worth it for a single-site independent?

Usually yes, because one person does the follow-ups in between everything else. The easiest first step is a weekly due list from your existing readings, written by hand at first. Add drafted messages once you see how many customers it finds.

## A rule for every shop

Every tread reading should end in a decision: fitted today, followed up with the customer's own numbers, or checked at the next visit. Let AI write the follow-ups and sort the replies, and keep every judgement about a tyre with the technician.
