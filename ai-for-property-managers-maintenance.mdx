Monday, 8 a.m., at a property management firm with three people and about 400 units. Twenty-three maintenance messages came in over the weekend. A dripping tap. A broken intercom. A photo of water coming through a bathroom ceiling, sent Saturday night. Five separate messages from one building saying the heating is off. A complaint about a neighbour's music, which isn't maintenance at all. Mould behind a wardrobe. A stairwell light out. A door that won't lock.

The property manager reads all twenty-three, calls three tenants back for details, works out that the five heating messages are one boiler problem, finds a plumber who can go to the leak today, and writes work orders. By the time the urgent jobs are dispatched it's past eleven, and the leak has been running, on and off, since Saturday.

The work in that morning isn't fixing anything. It's reading, sorting, asking and routing: exactly the part that AI can do in seconds, around the clock, so the urgent job reaches the right contractor on Saturday night instead of Monday at eleven.

## Not all requests are equal

The first thing a good property manager does with a request is judge two things: how urgent is it, and how many people does it affect? The model can make the same first judgment, and flag anything it's unsure about for a person.

The top-right quadrant is where AI saves the most confusion. Five tenants reporting "no heating" in the same building within an hour aren't five jobs. They're one boiler, one contractor, and one message to all tenants in the building: "We know, a technician is on the way, expected by 3 p.m."

## What each request needs before it goes to a contractor

Most back-and-forth with tenants happens because the first message didn't contain what the contractor needs. A guided intake asks for it straight away, in plain language, with photos.

| Request type | Typical urgency | Trade | What to ask the tenant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water leak | Emergency if active | Plumber | Where is the water coming from? Is it still running? Can you turn off the water to that fixture? Photo |
| No heating or hot water | Urgent; building-wide if several reports | Heating engineer | Whole flat or one radiator? Any error on the boiler display? Others in the building affected? |
| Electrical fault | Urgent if sparking, burning smell or no power | Electrician | Which circuits? Has the breaker tripped? Any smell or heat? If in doubt, switch off and leave the room |
| Door or lock | Urgent if the unit can't be secured | Locksmith | Can the door be locked? Front door or internal? |
| Mould | Needs inspection, not a quick fix | Inspector or building surveyor | Where, how large, since when? Ventilation habits? Photo |
| Appliances | Routine unless unsafe | Appliance repair | Make and model, what exactly happens, photo of the rating plate |
| Common areas | Routine | Caretaker | Where exactly? Photo |
| Noise, neighbours | Not maintenance | Property manager | Route to the manager, not a contractor |

The intake also asks for access: when is someone home, can the contractor use a key, are there pets. That single question saves more wasted contractor visits than anything else.

The emergency path matters most. A model shouldn't dispatch an emergency plumber on its own authority at 2 a.m., but it should wake the right person with a clear summary and the tenant's photo, and tell the tenant what to do in the meantime: turn off the stopcock, move valuables, stay safe.

## What the contractor receives

A work order drafted from a guided intake looks very different from "leak, flat 3B, call tenant":

> **Emergency: active leak, 14 Elm Street, flat 3B (2nd floor).** Water coming through the bathroom ceiling since about 21:30 Saturday, from the flat above (4B). Tenant in 3B has put down buckets; photo attached. Tenant in 4B not yet reached; the building's main stopcock is in the basement, key in the caretaker box (code in your contractor notes). Access to 3B: tenant home all Sunday. Approved by on-call manager at 21:52; spend limit €500 without further approval.

Everything the plumber needs is in one message: what, where, since when, what's been done, how to get in, and how far they can go without calling back. That's the difference between a contractor who fixes the problem on the first visit and one who spends the first half hour finding the stopcock.

## Who pays?

Not every repair is the landlord's responsibility. Tenant-caused damage, items the tenant owns, or repairs that the lease assigns to the tenant need a different path. The model can flag likely cases from the description ("the tenant says their child flushed a toy") and the lease terms, but the decision stays with the property manager. Telling a tenant they'll be charged is a conversation for a person, not a chatbot.

## Keep tenants informed

Most follow-up calls from tenants ask one question: "Has anyone looked at my request?" Automatic updates answer it: request received with a reference, contractor booked for Thursday between 8 and 12, job completed, please let us know if the problem persists. Each update is drafted from the work order status and sent without the manager typing it.

Building-wide messages are just as useful: "The heating in your building is being repaired today; the engineer expects to finish by 3 p.m." One message prevents twenty calls.

## Tools that fit

Property management software such as Buildium, AppFolio, Rent Manager or Yardi Breeze, and in Europe tools with tenant apps and work order modules, handle requests, contractors and work orders. Many have added AI features for categorising requests. The layer described here fills the gaps: a guided intake that asks the right questions per problem type, grouping of duplicate reports, an emergency path to the on-call person, and plain-language updates to tenants.

Tenant data, photos from inside homes and access details are personal and security-sensitive. Keep them in systems with proper data agreements, share access information only with the assigned contractor, and delete photos when they're no longer needed.

## Questions property managers ask

### Can tenants still just call?

Of course. Calls can be transcribed and run through the same intake, or taken by a person who uses the same checklist. Many older tenants will always call, and they should get the same quality of triage.

### What if the model gets the urgency wrong?

It will, occasionally, which is why anything that might be an emergency (water, fire, gas, electricity, security) is treated as urgent until a person has looked. It's much better to over-escalate a dripping pipe than to under-escalate a burst one.

### How does it pick the contractor?

From your own list: preferred contractors per trade and building, their working hours and emergency availability. The model suggests; the manager approves, at least until the pattern is trusted for routine jobs.

### What about owners?

Owners want to know what's happening in their buildings without reading every ticket. The same structured data produces a short monthly summary per building: number of requests by type, response times, costs, recurring problems ("third heating failure this winter, boiler is 19 years old, recommend replacement quote"), and decisions needed. Approval thresholds for larger repairs can be built into the flow, so owners are asked at the right moment instead of surprised by an invoice.

### Does this work for small portfolios?

Yes. The fewer staff you have, the more a weekend of unsorted messages hurts. Even with fifty units, grouping duplicates and getting complete details up front saves real time.

## Rule of thumb

Ask tenants the right questions at the moment they report, group what belongs together, send emergencies to a person immediately, and let routine work orders flow to the right contractor with everything they need. Then tell tenants what's happening before they have to ask.
