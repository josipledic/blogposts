The first warm Saturday of spring. By ten in the morning, the workshop of a small bike shop has eleven bikes lined up by the door, the phone has rung twenty times, and the booking diary is full for three weeks. Half the bikes are e-bikes. Two need a motor diagnosis that requires the manufacturer's software, one needs a brake part that's on backorder, and a customer who "just needs a quick check before a tour tomorrow" is getting visibly frustrated.

Every bike shop knows this week. The work that was spread thinly over winter arrives all at once, just as sales pick up and the staff is busiest on the shop floor. There aren't more mechanics to hire; the trade has been short of skilled people for years. So the question is how to make the capacity you have go further: spread the demand, prepare each job before the bike arrives, and stop spending hours on the phone telling people when their bike will be ready.

E-bikes changed workshop economics. They're heavier, their motors and batteries need diagnostic software and manufacturer-specific parts, and their owners ride more and expect faster turnaround because the bike is their commute. A workshop that could handle spring with paper booking and good humour ten years ago now needs a plan.

## Plan the season, don't sit it out

The single most effective change has nothing to do with the workshop floor. It's getting service work to happen in February instead of April.

<Timeline title="A bike shop season with a plan" caption="The idea: move as much predictable work as possible out of the peak, and prepare the rest before bikes arrive.">
<Phase when="January" title="Know your customers">From the service history, list everyone whose bike had a service last spring, e-bikes due for their annual check, and bikes sold last year due for their first service.</Phase>
<Phase when="Early February" title="Invite early">A personal message to each: "Your bike was last serviced in April. Book in February and it's ready before the season, often with a quieter-month discount."</Phase>
<Phase when="February" title="Stock the likely parts">Brake pads, chains, tyres and common e-bike parts, based on last spring's jobs and the bikes booked in.</Phase>
<Phase when="March to June" title="Peak with lanes">Booked services, a same-day lane for quick fixes, and a separate slot for e-bike diagnostics.</Phase>
<Phase when="Throughout" title="Status updates">Customers get a message when the bike is checked in, when a quote is ready, and when it's done.</Phase>
<Phase when="July" title="Review">Which jobs took longer than estimated, which parts ran out, how many customers booked early. Adjust for next year.</Phase>
</Timeline>

The invitation works best when it's personal and specific:

> Hi Anna, your Riese & Müller got its last service with us in April 2025, and the Bosch system is due for its annual check. If you book in February, it'll be ready before the first warm weekend, and quiet-month services are 10% off. Pick a slot here, or reply with a day that suits you.

AI helps with every step that involves reading a lot of records and writing a lot of messages: finding the right customers in the service history, writing personal invitations that mention their bike, forecasting which parts to stock, and sending status updates without a mechanic stopping to type.

## Every job, prepared before the bike arrives

The second lever is intake. A booking that says "service, Saturday" tells the workshop almost nothing. A structured intake, done online or by message when the customer books, tells it what to expect.

| Job type | Typical workshop time | What intake should capture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Puncture and quick fixes | 15 to 30 min | Wheel size, tyre type, tubeless or not; whether it's urgent |
| Basic service | about 1 h | Bike type, last service, anything noticed: noises, shifting, brakes |
| Full service | 2 to 3 h | As above, plus wear parts the customer knows are tired, budget limit for extra parts |
| E-bike check and diagnosis | 30 min plus any repair | Motor system and model, battery age, error codes on the display, photos |
| Hydraulic brake bleed | about 45 min | Brake brand and model, symptoms: soft lever, noise, pulling |
| Suspension service | often sent to a specialist | Fork or shock model, when it was last serviced |

With that information, the workshop can plan the day by real time estimates rather than by the number of bikes, check parts before the bike arrives, and know which jobs need the diagnostic laptop. The model can guide the customer through the intake in plain language ("Does the display show a code? If so, a photo is perfect"), and turn the answers into a job card.

The quote message deserves a moment. "Your chain and cassette are worn; replacing them now avoids damaging the chainrings. Parts and labour €89. Reply YES to go ahead." A mechanic writes that in their head in two seconds and on a phone in three minutes. The model writes it from the mechanic's note in a second, and the customer approves by reply instead of by phone.

## The quick-fix lane

The customer with the flat tyre before tomorrow's tour doesn't need a full service slot in three weeks. They need twenty minutes today. Many shops keep a small daily capacity for quick fixes: punctures, brake adjustments, a slipped chain. The intake identifies those jobs, and the booking system sends them to the lane instead of the backlog. It's one of the best things a shop can do for its reputation in spring.

Eighty-odd hours in the peak is roughly two extra weeks of one mechanic, exactly when you can't hire one. And it comes with calmer customers, because the ones who booked early are happy and the ones in the peak know when their bike will be ready.

## Tools that fit

Bike shop and workshop software such as Bike Cloud, Lightspeed, Ascend or a general booking tool handles appointments and job cards; the e-bike systems (Bosch, Shimano, Yamaha and others) have their own diagnostic tools that stay firmly in the mechanic's hands. The AI layer connects to the booking and customer records: it runs intake, drafts job cards, estimates times from your own history, suggests parts to stock, and sends messages. Customer contact details are personal data: only send marketing invitations to customers who've agreed to hear from you.

## Questions bike shop owners ask

### Will customers really book in February?

Many will, if you ask them personally and early, and give them a reason: a ready bike on the first warm day, and perhaps a modest quiet-month price. The ones who don't are no worse off than before.

### Can AI diagnose e-bike problems?

No. It can collect the right information (system, error codes, symptoms, photos) and suggest what the mechanic might check first based on similar past jobs. Diagnosis needs the manufacturer's tools and a trained mechanic.

### How accurate are the time estimates?

As accurate as your history. Start with your standard times per job type, record actual times for a season, and let the estimates improve. Even rough estimates plan a day better than counting bikes.

### What about bikes bought online?

Direct-to-consumer bikes arrive in the workshop more and more often, sometimes with parts that are hard to source. Intake helps here too: the model asks for the brand, model and year, checks whether you've serviced that brand before and whether common parts are available, and flags the job before it blocks a stand for two weeks. Some shops price these jobs differently, and say so upfront.

### What about customers who just walk in?

Walk-ins can do the same intake on a tablet at the counter. Quick fixes go to the lane; everything else gets a real date instead of "sometime next week".

## Rule of thumb

Move predictable work out of the peak, prepare every job before the bike arrives, and let customers hear from you before they have to call. AI does the reading, writing and remembering; your mechanics fix bikes.
