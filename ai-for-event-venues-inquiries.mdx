Here's an email thread most event venues will recognise. A couple writes on a Sunday evening: "Hi, is your venue available next June for our wedding?" The venue replies on Monday with a friendly note and a 14-page PDF brochure. On Wednesday the couple asks which Saturdays are free. On Thursday the venue lists three. On Friday the couple says there'll be about 120 guests. The following Tuesday the venue explains that the barn seats 110 for dinner, 180 standing. The couple asks about the terrace. The venue asks about their budget. Twelve days and nine emails later, the couple mentions they've booked a tour somewhere else, "just to compare".

Nobody in that thread did anything wrong. Each reply answered the question that was asked. But none of them moved the conversation forward, and in the time it took to establish the date, the headcount and whether the space even fits, the couple had found two other venues that answered faster.

This post takes that thread apart and shows how AI can collapse it into one or two useful replies.

Weddings are the obvious example, but the same pattern runs through corporate events, conferences, birthday parties and Christmas parties. The client sends the same inquiry to several venues, compares the first useful answers, and books tours with the ones that seem to fit. Venues that take a week to establish the basics simply aren't on the shortlist.

## Teardown: where the nine emails went

| Round | What was asked or answered | What could have been in the first reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Is the venue free in June? | The free Saturdays in June, straight away |
| 2 | Brochure sent | Only the parts that matter for a wedding of their likely size |
| 3 | Which dates? | Already answered in round 1 |
| 4 | About 120 guests | Asked in round 1: "Roughly how many guests?" |
| 5 | Barn seats 110 seated, 180 standing | Capacity by room and layout, alongside the dates |
| 6 | What about the terrace? | Terrace option offered proactively for 120 seated in summer |
| 7 | What's your budget? | A price range for their date and size, so they can judge fit themselves |
| 8 | Budget answered | Not needed if the range was clear |
| 9 | Tour booked elsewhere | A link to book a viewing in the first reply |

Most of the nine emails exist because the first reply didn't ask the right questions or offer the right information. That's not a writing problem. It's an information problem: the person answering didn't have the calendar, the capacities and the price rules at hand, or didn't have time to put them together on a Monday morning.

## What qualifies a venue inquiry

A few facts decide whether an inquiry fits and what to offer. If the first reply gathers them, or answers them from what the client already said, the rest of the conversation is about the event, not the basics.

| Fact | Why it matters | How to get it without an interrogation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date, or how flexible it is | Availability and seasonal pricing | Offer the free dates around their preference |
| Number of guests | Which rooms and layouts work | Ask for a range, not an exact number |
| Type of event | Wedding, corporate, party, conference: different packages and rules | Usually clear from the message |
| Format | Seated dinner, standing reception, theatre, classroom | Suggest the typical format and ask if it fits |
| Time frame | Day, evening, overnight, setup and teardown | Offer your standard slots |
| Catering and extras | In-house, approved caterers, own catering allowed? | State your rule clearly |
| Budget | Whether your packages fit | Give a range for their date and size instead of asking |
| Decision process | Who decides and when | Offer a viewing; that's where you learn this |

The budget row matters most. Asking "what's your budget?" puts clients on guard. Showing "for 120 guests on a Saturday in June, our wedding packages start around €9,500" lets them judge fit themselves, and people who are out of range stop writing instead of taking up a tour slot.

## The workflow

<Flow title="From first inquiry to a booked viewing" caption="The model reads, checks the calendar and capacities, and drafts. The events manager approves. The viewing and the relationship stay entirely human.">
<Step title="Inquiry arrives" who="Client" time="any time">Website form, email, a wedding platform, a phone message. All in one inbox.</Step>
<Step title="Extract what's known" who="AI" tone="ai" time="seconds">Event type, date or date range, guest count, format, any budget or special wishes mentioned.</Step>
<Step title="Check fit" who="AI" tone="ai">Calendar availability around the date, which rooms and layouts fit the guest count, any rules (noise curfew, catering, minimum spend).</Step>
<Step title="Draft the reply" who="AI" tone="ai">Free dates, suitable spaces, a price range from your rules, one or two questions, and viewing slots.</Step>
<Step title="Events manager approves" who="Events manager" tone="human" time="2 min">Checks dates and prices, adjusts tone, sends. Unusual requests get a personal reply.</Step>
<Step title="Hold and follow up" who="AI" tone="ai">Optional provisional hold on the date for a few days. A friendly follow-up if there's no reply after two days.</Step>
</Flow>

Two details make this work. First, the calendar check has to include holds and options, not only confirmed bookings, or the reply will offer dates that are already promised. Second, the price range comes from your own rules (day of the week, season, room, guest count, minimum spend), written down once. The model calculates; it doesn't invent.

## Provisional holds without chaos

Many venues offer a provisional hold, an option on the date for a week or two while the client decides. Handled on paper, options expire without anyone noticing, or two clients end up with an option on the same Saturday. The assistant can place a hold automatically when a client asks, set the expiry, remind the client two days before it lapses, and release it cleanly if they don't confirm. A person approves the hold for peak dates. It's a small thing that removes one of the most awkward conversations in the business: telling a couple their date went to someone else.

The bigger number is the one you can't see in the calculation: the couples and companies who would have booked a viewing if the first reply had been useful. For a venue where one wedding is worth €10,000 or more, winning one extra booking a quarter pays for a lot.

To find out whether it's working, keep a simple list of every inquiry with four columns: when it arrived, when the first reply went out, how many emails it took to reach a viewing or a no, and whether it became a booking. Two months of that list tell you more about your sales process than any software dashboard.

## Tools that fit

Venue management and event CRM tools such as Tripleseat or Perfect Venue handle inquiries, calendars, proposals and contracts, and some add AI replies. Many small venues run on a shared calendar, email and a spreadsheet of prices. Either way, the AI layer needs three things: read access to the calendar with holds, your room capacities by layout, and your price rules. With those, it can draft qualified replies from any inbox and log every inquiry in one list, so you can finally see where your bookings come from.

Inquiries contain personal details and sometimes sensitive information (a memorial event, a family situation). Use a provider with a data processing agreement where GDPR applies, and in the EU make it clear when an automated assistant is replying.

## Questions venue owners ask

### Should we really put prices in the first reply?

A range, yes. It's the single most effective filter, and clients appreciate not having to ask. If your pricing is complex, give a typical range for their date and size and say the exact proposal follows the viewing.

### Won't a fast, structured reply feel impersonal for a wedding?

Not if it's warm, specific to their date and guest count, and signed by a real person. Couples remember the venue that answered on Sunday night with exactly what they needed. The personal part, walking the space together, is where it belongs: at the viewing.

### What about corporate clients who need a formal proposal?

The same first reply works: availability, suitable rooms and layouts, a price range per person or day rate, and a question or two. For corporate inquiries, offer a call instead of a viewing and send the formal proposal after.

### What if their date is already taken?

Say so in the first reply and offer the nearest free dates, a weekday alternative at a lower price, or a different space on the same day if you have one. Many couples and companies are more flexible than their first message suggests, especially when the alternative comes with a better price. A fast, honest "that Saturday is gone, but these three work" keeps the conversation alive; a slow one ends it.

### Can the assistant handle viewings too?

It can offer slots and book them in your calendar. The viewing itself is yours: that's where you learn what the client really wants, and where most bookings are won.

## Rule of thumb

Answer the questions the client hasn't asked yet. Every first reply should include free dates, spaces that fit, a price range and a way to book a viewing. Let the model assemble it from your calendar and rules, and spend your own time at the viewing, not in the email thread.
