AI can help a scaffolding contractor keep track of every scaffold that is standing right now: when it was last inspected, who it was handed over to, and how many weeks of hire it has run past the quote. In most firms those three facts live in three places, the inspection app, the job file and the invoicing system, and they drift apart. The useful setup is one register of live scaffolds, kept up to date by plain rules, with a language model that turns inspectors' notes and photos into the written report, drafts the hire extension letter and sorts the client's replies. People still inspect, decide and sign.

The legal clock is short, and the stakes are high.

## What the regulations ask for

[Regulation 12 of the Work at Height Regulations 2005](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/735/regulation/12/made) sets three things that matter for the office:

- **The 7-day rule.** A working platform used for construction work, where someone could fall 2 metres or more, must not be used unless it was inspected in that position within the previous 7 days. It also has to be inspected after assembly and after "exceptional circumstances", such as a storm.
- **A report, fast.** The inspector has to write the report before the end of the working period and give it, or a copy, to the person the inspection was done for within 24 hours.
- **What goes in it.** [Schedule 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/735/schedule/7/made) lists eight particulars: who it was for, the location, a description of the scaffold, the date and time, anything that could be a risk, what was done about it, what still needs doing, and the inspector's name and position.

The report has to stay on site until the construction work is finished and then at an office for 3 months. None of this is hard. It is just relentless when you have forty scaffolds up across a town and three inspectors.

## Where it breaks today

- **Nobody owns the list of what's standing.** The yard knows what went out, the inspectors know what they visited, accounts knows what was quoted. A scaffold that was meant to come down in May and is still up in August shows up in nobody's list.
- **Inspection notes typed twice.** The inspector writes "lift 3 board missing, toe board loose bay 6, fixed on site" in a notebook or app, and someone in the office turns it into a proper report in the evening.
- **Storm checks by memory.** After high winds, every scaffold needs an inspection before use. Working out which ones, and telling every client, is done by phone.
- **Hire extensions nobody invoices.** The quote covered eight weeks. Week nine to fourteen get billed late, argued about, or not at all, because nobody wrote to the client when the period ran out.
- **Client replies scattered.** "Can you strike on Friday?", "we need an extra lift" and "who signed this off?" come in by email, text and site visit, to whoever happened to be there.

## Where AI fits

**One register of live scaffolds.** Every job gets one record: address, description, handover date, quoted hire period, last inspection and next due date. Plain rules keep it current from the handover form, the inspection app and the strike notice. This part is ordinary logic, not a language model, and it has to be exactly right.

**A due list every morning.** The register lists every scaffold due for inspection in the next two days, grouped by area, so inspectors plan a round instead of a scramble. After a weather warning, it lists every scaffold standing in that area and marks them all as "inspect before use".

**Reports written from the inspector's notes.** The inspector records what they saw as a voice note or a few lines plus photos. The model fills in the Schedule 7 report: the eight particulars, the defects found, what was fixed and what still needs doing. The inspector reads it, corrects it and signs it on the phone before leaving site, and the client gets it the same day.

**Hire extension letters on time.** When a scaffold passes its quoted hire period, the system drafts a short letter to the client with the address, the dates, the agreed weekly rate from your quote and the question "strike, or keep it up?". Rates come from your quote, never from the model. Someone in the office approves each letter.

**Replies sorted.** Each reply is tagged: wants a strike date, wants an alteration, questions an invoice, safety concern. A safety concern goes to a person straight away. Strike requests come with real free slots from the crew plan.

## Where it goes wrong

<Callout title="The model never decides that a scaffold is safe" tone="warning">
A competent inspector inspects and signs. The model only writes up what the inspector recorded. It never marks a scaffold as fit for use, never removes a defect from a report, and any reply from a client that mentions damage, a missing tie, an alteration by another trade or someone working on a tagged-out scaffold goes to a person the same hour.
</Callout>

**Reports that read too well.** A model can make a thin note sound complete. Keep the inspector's original note and photos attached to every report, and set the rule that a report with no photo, no defect list and no inspector signature cannot be sent.

**Wrong scaffold, right words.** If two jobs share a street, a report can land on the wrong record. Make the inspector pick the job from the due list or scan the tag, never type the address.

**Rates and dates from the model.** The weekly rate, the quoted period and the handover date come from your quote and your handover form. If the model writes a number that isn't in the data it was given, the letter does not go out.

## Tools that fit

Start with what you have. Several scaffolding apps already handle digital inspections with scaff tags and photos, and some track hire periods too. If yours does both well, the missing pieces are usually the report drafted from a voice note, the extension letter and a reply inbox that sorts itself. A custom layer pays off when inspections, hire and invoicing sit in three systems that don't talk to each other, which is common in firms that grew one tool at a time.

## Questions contractors ask

### Can an inspection report be written by AI at all?

The inspection can't. The report can be drafted from what the inspector recorded, as long as the inspector reads it, corrects it and signs it. Regulation 12 cares that a competent person inspected the scaffold and that the report has the right particulars and reaches the client in time.

### Will clients accept a hire extension letter from a system?

They accept it more easily than a surprise invoice six weeks later. A short, polite letter with the address, the dates and the agreed rate, sent the week the period runs out, starts the "strike or keep it?" conversation while everyone still remembers the quote.

### Is this worth it for a small firm?

Usually yes, because the person writing up reports is often also the person chasing payment. The easiest first step is the register on its own, built by hand from the yard and the job files. You'll see in a week whether the problem is inspections, hire, or both.

## A rule for every scaffolding firm

Every scaffold that is standing should have one record that answers three questions: when was it last inspected, who has it, and are we being paid for this week? Let AI write the reports and the letters from that record, and keep every judgement about safety with your inspectors.
