AI can write the first draft of a loss adjuster's preliminary report: read the site visit notes, voice memos, photos and the insured's first documents, set out the circumstances and the cause as reported, map the loss against the policy schedule and wording clause by clause, show how the reserve was built, and list every document still needed from the insured. The adjuster still decides the cover position, sets the reserve and signs the report. What changes is that the adjuster edits a structured draft the evening after the site visit instead of writing it from a notebook three days later.

Commercial loss adjusting is high value per file, and the preliminary report is where the insurer first decides how much to hold and whether the claim is covered.

Crawford is one listed firm among many UK adjusters, so its figures show the shape of the work rather than the size of the market. Its 10-K reports $1.266 billion of group revenue before reimbursements for 2025. In the UK, high-frequency, low-severity cases fell while revenue rose on higher-value third-party administration work. Larger, more complex files are the end of the market where a report takes hours and a wrong reserve costs an insurer real money.

## The legal clock behind every report

The [Insurance Act 2015, section 13A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/13A), inserted by the Enterprise Act 2016 and in force since 4 May 2017, makes it an implied term of every insurance contract that the insurer pays sums due on a claim within a reasonable time. A reasonable time includes a reasonable time to investigate and assess the claim. What counts as reasonable depends on the type of insurance, the size and complexity of the claim, compliance with regulatory rules and factors outside the insurer's control.

For commercial policies, [section 16A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/4/section/16A) lets the parties contract out of that term only for breaches that are not deliberate or reckless, and only if the transparency requirements are met. So the investigation is part of the clock, not a pause from it, and the adjuster's file is what shows whether the time taken was reasonable. A preliminary report that lists exactly what is outstanding and who owes it is the best evidence an insurer has.

For commercial fire losses, there is a second reporting duty. Member insurers of RISCAuthority require qualifying commercial fire losses over £100,000 to be entered into its Large Loss Database, which was relaunched on 13 July 2026 with around 40 fields instead of more than 400, according to the [Chartered Institute of Loss Adjusters](https://cila.co.uk/new-riscauthority-large-loss-database-supporting-better-claims-insights/). Most of those fields are already in the adjuster's report.

## What the preliminary report has to answer

Every insurer client has its own template and deadline, but the questions repeat:

| Section | What the insurer needs to know |
|---|---|
| Circumstances | What happened, when, where, who reported it, what the adjuster saw on site |
| Cause | Reported cause, evidence for it, whether a forensic expert is needed |
| Cover | Which section and peril respond, relevant conditions, exclusions and endorsements, any notification or warranty issues |
| Sum insured | Declared values against what is on site, possible underinsurance and average |
| Reserve | Building, contents, stock and business interruption elements, each with its basis |
| Mitigation and recovery | Emergency works, salvage, third-party recovery prospects |
| Outstanding | Documents and information requested from the insured, with dates |

## Where the time and the risk sit

**The notes are in four formats.** Handwritten notes from site, a voice memo from the car, 200 photos, and a folder of documents the insured's broker emailed over. Turning that into a coherent narrative is the slow part.

**The wording is long and the schedule is specific.** A commercial combined policy runs to 80 pages plus endorsements. The cover analysis depends on a handful of clauses that have to be found and quoted correctly every time.

**Reserves drift.** The first reserve is set on limited information. If the report does not show how it was built, nobody can tell later which assumption moved when the figure changed.

## What the report assistant does

**Turn site material into a draft narrative.** It transcribes voice memos, reads photos and notes, and drafts circumstances and cause in the insurer's template, marking every statement as observed, reported by the insured or assumed.

**Map the loss against the policy.** It reads the schedule, wording and endorsements and lists the clauses that look relevant, quoting each one with its page, and the questions they raise: a security condition after a theft, an unoccupancy clause, a sum insured that looks low against the stock records.

**Show how the reserve is built.** It sets out each head of claim with its basis (repair estimate, stock records, gross profit from the last accounts) and keeps a history of every change with the reason.

**List what is outstanding.** It drafts the information request to the insured or broker and tracks replies, so the next report starts from what changed.

The hours matter twice. Adjusting is often billed on time or on fee scales agreed with the insurer, so report writing is margin. And a report that reaches the insurer days earlier, with a reserve it can trust, is what the claims manager notices when choosing which firm gets the next large loss.

## Where it goes wrong

<Callout title="The assistant drafts. The adjuster decides." tone="warning">
The cover position, the reserve and any recommendation to decline or pay are professional judgements the adjuster makes for the insurer. The model shows the clauses, the evidence and the arithmetic. A person decides, signs and answers for the report.
</Callout>

**Policy wordings vary more than templates suggest.** An endorsement or a manuscript clause can change everything. The assistant has to quote the wording it relied on, and the adjuster checks that the right version of the policy is on file.

**Personal and commercial data.** Files contain the insured's accounts, staff details and sometimes police or fire service reports. Run the assistant where the insurer's and your firm's data rules allow, with access limited to the file handler.

**The insured's account is not evidence.** The draft must keep what was reported apart from what was seen and what was verified, because that distinction decides fraud referrals and recoveries later.

## Tools that fit

Most adjusting firms run files in a claims management system and write reports in Word templates from each insurer. The cheapest first step is an assistant that reads the file folder and writes the draft into that template, so nothing about the report's delivery changes. Once that works, the same file data can prefill the RISCAuthority Large Loss Database entry for qualifying fire losses.

## Questions loss adjusters ask about AI and reports

### How long does an insurer have to pay a commercial claim in the UK?

There is no fixed number of days. Section 13A of the Insurance Act 2015 implies a term that the insurer pays sums due within a reasonable time, which includes a reasonable time to investigate and assess the claim. What is reasonable depends on the type of insurance, the size and complexity of the claim, regulatory rules and factors outside the insurer's control.

### Can AI set the reserve on a claim?

It should not. AI can lay out each head of claim with its basis and keep the history of changes, but the reserve recommendation is the adjuster's and the reserve itself is the insurer's decision.

### Which commercial fire losses have to be reported to the RISCAuthority Large Loss Database?

According to the Chartered Institute of Loss Adjusters, member insurers require qualifying commercial fire losses exceeding £100,000 to be entered. The database was relaunched on 13 July 2026 with around 40 data fields.
