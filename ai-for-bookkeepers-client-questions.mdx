Monday morning in a two-person bookkeeping practice. The inbox has 31 new messages. "Did you get my receipts?" "Why does QuickBooks say I made $40,000 when there's $6,000 in the bank?" "Who still owes me money?" "Can I put my phone on the business?" Different clients, the same questions as last Monday, and the Monday before.

Each answer takes three to ten minutes. Most of them are sitting in the books you already keep. That's the case for an AI assistant in a bookkeeping practice: not to replace the bookkeeper, but to stop the bookkeeper from being a human search engine for data the client could see themselves if someone explained it in plain words.

The profession is moving that way anyway. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects employment of bookkeeping, accounting and auditing clerks to [fall 6% between 2024 and 2034](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/bookkeeping-accounting-and-auditing-clerks.htm), mostly because routine entry work is being automated. The bookkeepers who do well will be the ones clients see as the person who explains the numbers, not the one who looks them up.

## Count before you build

Before choosing any tool, spend two weeks tagging every client message with one of a handful of categories. It sounds tedious. It takes a minute a day, and it tells you exactly where your time goes.

In most practices I'd expect a pattern like this: a handful of categories cover the large majority of messages, and the long tail is where your expertise is actually needed. The top three bars are almost pure lookup. Nobody needs a bookkeeper's judgment to say whether a file arrived.

## The ten questions, ranked

Here's how I'd rank the usual suspects by how often they come in, with what an assistant can do for each and where it has to stop.

### 1. "Did you get my documents?"

The most frequent and the least interesting. The answer is in your portal or document inbox. An assistant with read access can reply instantly: "Yes, we received 14 receipts on Tuesday and your March bank statement for the Chase account. We're still missing the statement for the Amex card." This one can be answered automatically, because the only thing it reports is what's in your own system.

### 2. "Who hasn't paid me?"

Clients ask this because they don't open the accounts receivable report, even when they have access. The assistant can pull the aging list from QuickBooks Online or Xero, sort it by amount and age, and offer to draft polite payment reminders to the client's customers. The list goes out automatically. The reminders to the client's customers should be approved by the client, since they're going out in the client's name.

### 3. "Why is my profit different from my bank balance?"

This is the most valuable question on the list, because it's where clients start to understand their own business. The answer is always some mix of the same items: unpaid invoices, owner draws, loan principal repayments, inventory purchases, tax set aside, and timing. An assistant can build the explanation from the client's own numbers:

> Your profit for the first quarter was $40,200. Your bank balance went up by only $6,100. The difference: $18,500 of invoices your customers haven't paid yet, $9,000 you took as owner draws, $4,200 in loan repayments (only the interest counts as an expense), and $2,400 of stock you bought but haven't sold.

I'd have a bookkeeper review this before it's sent, at least for the first few months. The numbers come from the books, but the explanation is interpretation, and getting it slightly wrong confuses more than it helps.

### 4. "What do you need from me this month?"

The reverse of question 1, and often never asked because clients don't know to ask it. Every month there's a pile of uncategorized transactions only the client can explain. The assistant can turn the list into questions the client recognises: "On March 14 you paid $89.90 to Amazon. What was it for? If it was personal, just say so." Grouping helps: "Three payments to Shell. Fuel for the van?" A single reply from the client clears ten lines in the books.

### 5. "When is my next deadline?"

Sales tax filings, payroll deposits, quarterly estimated tax payments in the US, VAT returns in the UK. The dates are known in advance for each client, so this answer can be automatic. It works even better turned around: a short reminder a week before each deadline, sent without anyone asking.

### 6. "Can I see my numbers?"

"Can you send me my P&L?" usually means "Am I doing okay?" Sending the report is easy. Adding three sentences on top that say what changed ("Revenue is up 12% on last March, mostly from the Henderson project. Materials costs rose faster than revenue. Cash is healthy for the next two months.") is where the value is. Draft for review.

### 7. "Can you pay this bill, or add a new employee to payroll?"

These are tasks, not questions. The assistant's job is structured intake: confirm what's being asked, collect what's missing (a new hire's W-4 and start date, the bill's due date and approval), and create a task in your practice management tool. It shouldn't initiate payments on its own. A person does that.

### 8. "How do I upload, connect or log in?"

How to reconnect a bank feed, forward receipts by email, or reset a portal password. These answers belong in a short help library that the assistant quotes from. They can be answered automatically, and they're the ones clients most appreciate getting at 9 p.m. on a Sunday.

### 9. "Can I expense this?"

Now we're in different territory. "Can I put my phone on the business?" sounds simple, but the correct answer depends on the client's situation and, often, on tax rules. If your practice has a written position on common items, the assistant can share it with a clear note to confirm with you. Anything beyond that goes to a person, or to the client's accountant.

### 10. "How much tax will I owe?"

The assistant can say what the books show: profit to date, and how much the client has set aside if you track that. It should not estimate a tax bill. That's tax preparation and advice, and it belongs with whoever prepares the client's return. Route it, every time.

Batching the review matters. If the bookkeeper checks drafts twice a day instead of reacting to each message as it arrives, the interruptions stop, and that's often worth as much as the minutes saved.

<Callout title="Bookkeeping answers, not tax advice" tone="warning">
The assistant reports what's in the books and explains it. It doesn't decide what's deductible, estimate tax bills, or respond to tax authority letters. Put that boundary in writing, in the assistant's instructions and in your engagement letter. If a message touches tax, the assistant says a person will reply and routes it. In the UK, remember that a practising bookkeeper also has to be [supervised for anti-money laundering](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/money-laundering-regulations-who-needs-to-register), and an automated assistant doesn't change any of those obligations.
</Callout>

## What it needs to work

**Read access to the books.** QuickBooks Online and Xero both have APIs that make the common reports available: aging, profit and loss, balance sheet, uncategorized transactions. Read-only is enough for everything above, and it's much safer.

**Your document inbox.** Whatever you use for receipts and statements, whether that's a portal like TaxDome or Karbon, Dext, Hubdoc, or a shared folder.

**A written answer library.** Twenty to forty short entries covering how-to questions, your positions on common expense questions, and your standard wording. This is also the part most practices never write down, and it's useful even without AI: new staff learn from it too.

**A deadline calendar per client.** Most practice management tools already have one.

## The case against

I'll be honest about when this isn't worth it. If you have ten clients and they mostly call you, an assistant is overkill; a shared FAQ page and a monthly email with each client's numbers will do more. If your clients value the personal relationship above everything else and pay accordingly, fast automated answers may matter less than a real voice.

And if your books are three months behind, the assistant will answer questions from stale numbers. Get current first.

## Questions bookkeepers ask

### Will clients know they're talking to an AI?

They should. Say so in the first message and in your engagement letter. In the EU, telling people they're interacting with an AI system is a legal requirement under [Article 50 of the AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) since August 2026. In practice, clients care more about getting a right answer quickly than about who typed it.

### What if it gives a wrong number?

For automatic answers, the numbers come straight from the accounting software, so the risk is stale or miscategorized data, not invented figures. Keep the automatic list to lookups, have drafts checked, and make it easy for clients to reply "that doesn't look right".

### Can I charge for this?

Some practices fold it into a higher monthly tier with "same-day answers". Others use it to take on more clients without hiring. Either way, don't sell it as a replacement for talking to you.

### Which software do I need?

Nothing exotic. Your accounting software, your portal or practice management tool, and a language model connected through their APIs. The effort goes into the answer library and deciding which categories are safe, not into the technology.

## Rule of thumb

If you've answered the same question five times this month, write the answer down once. If the answer lives in the books, let the assistant fetch it. If the answer needs your judgment, keep it yours.
