Picture a three-person studio refreshing twelve rooms of a boutique hotel. The spec schedule has 212 line items from 17 suppliers: headboards, bedside lamps, curtains, desks, chairs, rugs, mirrors. The hotel closes for a fortnight in week 31, and the installers are booked.

In week 18, a workshop emails that the headboard fabric is delayed by three weeks. The message lands in a mailbox with 140 unread emails. The schedule says "ordered, confirmed", because that was true last month. Nobody connects the email to its row until week 28, when the hotel manager asks, almost in passing, whether the headboards will be there.

Nobody made a mistake in the usual sense. A date changed in a message that never reached the document everybody trusts. That's the narrow job I'd give AI in a design studio: read what suppliers send, update the schedule row it belongs to, and raise a hand when a date no longer fits the install week. The designer still decides what to do about it, and the client still approves any substitution.

A studio of three people, or one, has no procurement department. It has a spreadsheet, a shared inbox and whoever remembers. The waits are long, and the clock often starts later than you'd think: ProcureWave says the production time doesn't begin until the final sample is approved. The two lead-time figures come from vendor-side sources, not a survey, so read them as ballpark. Even so, a single late approval in month two can turn into a missed opening in month nine.

The truth about one line item lives in several places at once:

- **The order confirmation**, a PDF or email saying what the supplier agreed to build and in which week.
- **The shipping notice**, with a carrier, a carton count and an estimated arrival.
- **A message from the sales rep**, sometimes by email, sometimes by WhatsApp, sometimes by phone.
- **The supplier portal**, if there is one.
- **The receiving report** from the warehouse or forwarder.
- **The spec schedule**, which is the document everyone reads and is only as current as the last person who typed into it.

The schedule records what someone typed. The suppliers' messages are what changed. The gap between the two is where the surprises live.

## What the assistant reads, and what it writes

Think of each schedule row as a handful of fixed fields: item, supplier, quantity, finish, PO number, confirmed ship week, needed-on-site week, status and who approved what. Incoming messages change those fields. Four kinds cover most of what arrives.

| Message | What the AI extracts | What it updates | What a person decides |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Order confirmation (PDF or email) | PO number, items, finish, size, quantity, price, confirmed ship week | Status "confirmed" and the ship week | Any difference from the approved spec: finish, size, quantity, price |
| Shipping notice or tracking email | Carrier, number of cartons, estimated arrival | Status "in transit" and the arrival date | Only whether arrival is later than the needed-on-site week |
| Delay or change email ("fabric delayed three weeks") | New date, stated reason, any alternative the supplier offers | Ship week and a risk flag | Wait, expedite, substitute or move the install, and what the client hears |
| Receiving report or delivery photos | Cartons counted, damage noted, items short | Status "received", "short" or "damaged" | Claim, reorder, or accept with a repair |

Reading a PDF in seventeen layouts and matching it to the right row is the job a [furniture store](/posts/ai-for-furniture-stores-delivery-scheduling) does with its own suppliers' confirmations, seen from the other side of the counter. Language models handle this messy-document work well.

The check that earns its keep is plain arithmetic: confirmed ship week, plus transit, plus a buffer for receiving and inspection, against the week the item has to be on site. If the sum lands after the needed week, the row turns red. If it leaves less than a week of slack, amber. The model reads the emails. The date comparison should be ordinary code, because that's the part that must never be creative.

</Flow>

Three rules keep it honest. **It never overwrites silently**: every change shows what it replaced and where the new value came from. **It never invents a date**: if a message states none, the row says "no date given" and a chase email is drafted. And **it never talks to the client**. The designer reads everything that reaches them.

## When the sofa is late: the decision that stays with you

A flagged row leaves four options: wait, expedite, substitute, or move the install. The assistant can lay out the options and what each does to the schedule. It can't choose, because each one touches the design or the contract.

Substitution is the dangerous one. A different fabric or chair changes the look, the dimensions, often the lead time, and in commercial projects it can change compliance. In Britain, for example, VisitBritain's Pink Book says [hotels and B&Bs need furniture that meets commercial fire safety standards](https://www.visitbritain.org/business-advice/pink-book/fire-safety-furniture-and-furnishings), while the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 cover only upholstered furniture supplied for domestic use. A catalogue substitute can silently move from one category to the other. Other countries have their own rules, so the habit is what matters: check the substitute's fire documentation before the client says yes, and ask the fire authority if unsure.

## Receiving: where claims are won or lost

Everything above is wasted if a delivery is signed for carelessly. Many carriers won't cover damage that isn't noted on the proof of delivery. Redwood Logistics, a freight advisory firm, says [most carriers require concealed damage claims within 5 to 15 days of delivery](https://www.redwoodlogistics.com/insights/4-tips-for-concealed-damage-freight-claims), varying by carrier, and advises writing "pending further inspection" in the signature box if the driver won't wait. Check your carrier's actual terms.

## Cheaper fixes first

Not every studio needs this. Where I'd stay away from AI:

- **Under about 40 line items, or one supplier per project.** A spreadsheet and a 20-minute review every Friday is enough.
- **Suppliers with portals or tracking links.** Use their tracking.
- **Dedicated FF&E software.** Studio Designer, Programa and Fohlio all aim at spec schedules and purchase orders, and Fohlio targets large commercial and hospitality projects. If you're on one, ask what it already does with incoming mail before building anything.
- **The date comparison.** A spreadsheet formula does it. No model required.
- **Chasing.** A recurring reminder per supplier ("Thursday: confirm ship week") fixes more than you'd expect.

AI earns its place when confirmations and shipping notices arrive as PDFs and emails in a dozen layouts, your team retypes them, and projects overlap so nobody holds the whole picture in their head. ProcureWave says a spreadsheet copes with a handful of rooms but not with a few hundred line items across dozens of suppliers, because nobody can see committed spend against budget in real time. That's a vendor's view. My own guess is that the threshold sits past a hundred items per project. Count the hours you lose and you'll know.

## What tracking costs, and what a miss costs

**Assumptions, not measurements.** 200 line items, about five supplier messages per item over its life (confirmation, shipping notice, a question, a change, delivery), and two minutes per message to read it, find the row and type the update. That's 1,000 messages and about 33 hours of reading and typing per project, before anyone chases anything.

Now take the twelve rooms from the opening, held closed for two extra weeks at an average rate of €110 a night. 12 rooms × 14 nights × €110 is about €18,500 in room revenue the hotel doesn't earn. That's revenue, not profit, and who carries it depends on the contracts. It's still the number that makes the hotel manager's question in week 28 expensive.

The assistant's own cost is small. Model fees for reading about 1,000 messages should be a few euros per project, my estimate, not a quote. The work is in the setup: a first version that reads a dedicated mailbox, handles your three biggest suppliers' layouts and writes into your spreadsheet or tool takes a few days to build, plus two or three weeks side by side with the manual process on a live project.

The recurring cost is a person: ten to fifteen minutes a day on exceptions. If it needs two hours, the inputs are too messy, and the fix lies with the suppliers or the schedule, not a better model.

## Client data, trade prices and NDAs

Supplier messages carry personal data: the client's name on the reference line, the delivery address, the floor, sometimes notes such as "key with the neighbour". In a private home there may be alarm or door codes. All of it is personal data under the GDPR and UK GDPR.

An AI service that receives it is a processor. You need a data processing agreement under [Article 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/), clarity on where the data is processed, and a guarantee that it isn't used to train models. Keeping a project on schedule falls under performing the contract, [Article 6(1)(b)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/).

Three habits matter more than the legal text:

- **Don't point the assistant at your whole inbox.** A dedicated orders address, or a forwarding rule for supplier mail, means it sees supplier messages and nothing else.
- **Keep door and alarm codes out of the schedule.** The model doesn't need them to read a confirmation.
- **Check your contracts before project data goes anywhere.** Net prices and trade discounts are commercially sensitive, and hotel groups and private clients often have confidentiality clauses.

One question isn't a model question at all: whose name is on the purchase order, yours or the client's? That decides who carries payment risk, warranty questions and, depending on where you work, tax treatment. Settle it with your accountant and your contracts before you automate anything around it.

## Questions studio owners ask

### How do interior designers track furniture orders?

The usual answer is a spec schedule: a spreadsheet or a purpose-built tool with a status for every line item. What differs is whether anything reads the incoming mail. In most studios, a person does, between everything else.

### Can AI read supplier order confirmations?

Yes, and it's where I'd start. Take the last 20 confirmations from your biggest supplier, have a model extract the fields, and compare with what you would have entered. If most match, a person only checks the exceptions.

### Will it chase suppliers for me?

It can draft the email: item, PO number, the date you were given and the date you need. A person sends it, at least until you trust the drafts. It doesn't negotiate.

## Where I'd start

Pick one live project and your three slowest suppliers. Forward their messages to a separate mailbox. Each Monday, have the assistant list the rows where confirmed date plus transit no longer fits the needed-on-site week, and compare that with what you'd have caught yourself. If the list is right most weeks, add the row updates. Keep every client-facing message manual.

My rule of thumb: the schedule should be at least as current as your inbox, and nothing should reach a client that you haven't read.
