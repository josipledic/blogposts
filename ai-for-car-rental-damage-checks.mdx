A customer drops a hatchback at the key box at 7:50 a.m., ten minutes before the office opens. Two days later the bill arrives: 380 euros for a scratched rear bumper. The customer writes back that the scratch was already there at pickup. You pull the handover sheet. It shows a car outline with an X on the rear bumper and the word "scratch". No photo, no size, nothing to show it's the same mark.

Is it? Nobody can tell. Not you, not the customer, not a judge. You either absorb the repair or charge someone who feels cheated, and who will say so in a review.

That's the typical shape of a rental damage dispute. The damage is usually real. What's missing is proof of when it happened. The big rental brands are trying to fix this with AI scanners, and the results have been mixed. For a fleet of 10 to 50 cars, I think the scanner is the wrong first purchase. A disciplined photo routine solves most of the problem for almost no money, and it makes any AI tool you add later work better. Here's how I'd compare the three options.

## Disputes are about proof, not damage

The consumer side says the same. The European Consumer Centres Network (ECC-Net) received [6,016 complaints about car rental companies in 2025](https://www.eccnet.eu/news/ecc-nets-recommendations-address-unfair-car-rental-practices), and lists unreasonably high damage charges and disputes about the condition of the vehicle among the recurring problems. In its [position paper](https://www.eccnet.eu/publication/driving-change-stronger-consumer-protection-car-rental-sector), the biggest obstacle in these cases is the customer's lack of proof, especially when the car is returned outside office hours. Its proposed fix: inspections in the customer's presence at pick-up and drop-off.

Consumer advisers already tell renters what to do about it. Germany's European Consumer Centre (EVZ) [advises renters](https://www.evz.de/aktuelles/pressemitteilungen/mietwagen-urlaub/) to photograph the car, ask for a return protocol and avoid key boxes where they can. A customer who follows that advice arrives with better evidence than your paper diagram.

The law depends on where you operate. In Germany, a rental company that claims damages has to show the car was undamaged at handover and that the damage arose during the rental, which is why a missing or vague handover protocol [usually hurts the lessor](https://www.haufe.de/recht/weitere-rechtsgebiete/verkehrsrecht/schadensersatz-mietwagen-ohne-uebergabeprotokoll_212_634038.html). Claims for damage to a rented item also expire six months after the car comes back, under [§ 548 BGB](https://dejure.org/gesetze/BGB/548.html). Good evidence and a prompt, documented bill both matter. Elsewhere, check local rules.

## Three ways to document a car

You have three realistic options. They differ less in price than in what you can show afterwards.

| | Paper diagram | Phone photo routine | AI damage detection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it records | Car outline with marked damage, signed by both sides | 20 to 24 timestamped photos in a fixed order, plus dashboard and fuel | The same photos or a scan, compared automatically with the previous set |
| What it costs to start | Printing | A phone you already own and a shared folder, or your rental software | Subscription or per-inspection fee; fixed scanners need an installation quote |
| Time per handover (my rough estimate) | 2 minutes | 4 to 6 minutes | 3 to 5 minutes, plus reviewing the flags |
| Strength in a dispute | Weak: no size, no depth, no proof of date | Strong: the customer sees the same photos | Strong if a person checks the flags, weak if charges are automatic |
| Main weakness | A cross on an outline can't separate a scuff from a dent | Depends on discipline; rain, dirt and dusk ruin photos | Reflections, dirt and old marks cause false flags; needs good earlier photos |
| Best fit | Signature page and fallback | Any small fleet. Start here | Large fleets, high volume, or review as the bottleneck |

Keep the paper diagram: it's the page the customer signs, and it works when a phone battery dies. It just can't carry a dispute alone. The photo routine is the camera plus consistency, and nothing about it is clever. That's the point.

AI damage detection comes in two shapes: a fixed scanner, a drive-through gate with cameras and dedicated lighting, or phone-based capture, where software compares the new photo set with the last one. Either way, it highlights differences. It doesn't know whose fault they are.

## What the Hertz scanner dispute actually shows

Hertz is the cautionary example, so it's worth being precise. Hertz rolled out scanners from the vendor UVeye at US airport locations. Customers began posting bills for small marks. One renter reported [a $440 charge for a one-inch scuff on a wheel](https://techxplore.com/news/2025-07-hertz-customer-ai-atlanta-airport.html). In a letter to Hertz, US Senator Richard Blumenthal described [a $195 charge for a dent that cost $80 to repair](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/icymi-blumenthal-demands-answers-from-hertz-about-ai-rental-car-inspections), with the other $115 labelled as fees for detecting, estimating and processing. The House Oversight Committee [also wrote to Hertz](https://oversight.house.gov/release/mace-investigates-use-of-artificial-intelligence-for-car-rental-damage-assessments/) in August 2025.

Hertz's side: according to a spokesperson quoted by [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hertz-uveye-ai-tech-rental-car-inspections/), more than 97% of the 675,000 rentals scanned so far showed no billable damage, and disputed cases are reviewed by people. It still leaves up to roughly 20,000 customers with a flagged car, and many of the complaints were about what happened next: automatic charges, processing fees on top of the repair cost, and an appeal route that started with a chatbot.

So the lesson isn't that scanning is bad. A scanner that finds a real dent at 2 a.m. is valuable. The lesson is that the process around the model decides whether customers trust it. Sixt runs a similar system it calls [Car Gate](https://www.sixt.com/help-center/articles/car-gate/), a drive-through scanner with cameras, sensors and lighting at selected locations. These are built for thousands of rentals a month, at sites with staff to review every flag. A ten-car rental has neither, but it has someone at the counter who can show the customer the photos.

## The photo routine I'd start with

This is deliberately boring. It takes a few minutes per handover, and it's the foundation under everything else.

<Checklist title="A handover photo set that holds up in a dispute" items={[
  "All four corners at a diagonal, so each shows two panels",
  "Each panel straight on: bonnet, roof, boot lid, doors, both bumpers",
  "All four wheels and rims, close up, because kerb damage is easy to miss and easy to dispute",
  "Windscreen and other glass, looking for chips and cracks",
  "Every existing mark twice: a wide shot for location, a close-up with a coin or ruler for scale",
  "Interior: seats, boot, dashboard, and anything the customer might be blamed for later",
  "Odometer, fuel or charge level and any warning lights in a single photo",
  "Same order every time, with the contract number and the plate in the file name",
  "Taken with the customer present, and sent to them by SMS or email before they drive off",
  "Repeated at return in the customer's presence, in the same order, in daylight or under lights"
]} />

Two details matter more than the list itself.

**The customer gets the photos immediately.** With the pickup set on their phone, they can't claim you never documented the bumper, and you can't be accused of adding marks later. That's what ECC-Net asks for.

**After-hours returns need their own flow.** The key box is where proof is easiest to lose, and it's the case ECC-Net singles out. Either require staffed returns, or send a link at pickup so the customer uploads the return set when they park. Write into your terms that the car counts as returned when the photos arrive. The upload also gives the customer proof of the car's state.

Hand over clean cars. Dirt and wet panels hide scratches from people and cameras alike, and a 60-second walk-around video with spoken commentary is a cheap second layer. [Moving companies use customer walkthrough videos](/posts/ai-for-moving-companies-inventory) for estimates, and the same idea works for a car.

## Where AI adds something on top

Once the photo sets exist, software can do work that is tedious for people.

It can compare the pickup set with the return set, panel by panel, and list the differences. It can assemble the evidence packet for a real claim: the two photos side by side, the damaged area marked, a repair estimate draft, a polite letter to the customer. And it can rank the flags, so you look first at the new dent and not at a reflection on the roof.

Vendors in this area include UVeye for fixed scanners and Inspektlabs and Ravin AI for phone-based capture. According to [Inspektlabs](https://inspektlabs.com/damage-detection), which sells AI inspection software to rental fleets, its detection is 95 to 99% accurate across 163 vehicle parts, and its [rental product](https://inspektlabs.com/car-rental) lets customers photograph the car themselves via a link sent by SMS or email. That's a vendor claim, and I haven't found an independent test of it. Also, accuracy per detection is not accuracy per car: even a model that is right 98 times in 100 will be wrong about two decisions in every hundred, and a busy rental makes thousands of decisions a month. That's why a person has to look at every flag before money changes hands.

After a few months of cases, treat the log like a laundry treats its complaint file. [Commercial laundries link complaints to machines and textiles](/posts/ai-for-laundries-quality-control), and a rental can link damage to a car, a location, a handover staff member or a type of customer. Wheel damage on one model, or three disputes from one branch, points to a fix that no scanner will find.

The photo routine isn't free. With 240 rentals, there are 480 handovers a month. At three extra minutes each, that's 24 hours of staff time, about €600 at €25 an hour. If the routine turns even half of those unprovable cases into won claims or disputes that never start, it roughly breaks even in direct money. Fewer arguments and fewer bad reviews come on top.

An AI layer adds a fee. I haven't seen published prices for small fleets, so get two or three quotes and put your own figure into the calculation.

## Data protection and fair charging

Photos of a rental car, tied to a contract and a plate, are personal data about your customer. A few rules of thumb for EU businesses:

- **Keep them only as long as you need them.** GDPR's storage limitation principle in [Article 5](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/) means you need a retention period. A reasonable starting point is the claim window, six months after return in Germany, plus any open dispute. Longer retention for accidents or insurance cases is a question for your lawyer.
- **Keep people out of the frame.** Faces, ID documents and a customer's belongings don't belong in damage photos. Retake the shot.
- **Sign a processing agreement with any vendor** that stores or analyses the photos, as [Article 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/) requires, and ask where the images are hosted.
- **Tell customers** in your privacy notice that you photograph the car at handover and return, and how long you keep the photos.

<Callout title="Never charge on the machine's say-so" tone="warning">
A charge decided by software alone, with no person looking at it, is the pattern that got Hertz into trouble. In the EU there's a legal angle too: [GDPR Article 22](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/) gives people a right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that significantly affects them, with exceptions such as contract necessity, and with safeguards like human intervention and the right to contest. Whether an automated damage charge falls under it is a question for a lawyer. Having a person genuinely review and approve every charge, and showing the customer the before and after photos, keeps you on safer ground and is simply fairer.
</Callout>

## When AI is the wrong tool

Under 15 cars, with the same two people doing most handovers, a scanner or an AI inspection subscription is hard to justify.

If your disputes come from after-hours returns, the fix is a return flow, not a model. If they come from unclear terms about who pays what, the fix is a clearer contract.

## Questions rental owners ask

### Do I need an AI damage scanner for a small rental fleet?

Probably not yet. Run a photo routine for 60 days and count disputes and write-offs. If cases pile up faster than you can compare photos by eye, AI comparison starts to earn its fee.

### How many photos should a handover include?

There's no legal number. The set above is 20 to 24 photos. The goal is that someone who wasn't there can tell the car's condition from the set alone.

### Who has to prove the damage in a rental dispute?

It varies by country. In Germany, the rental company has to show the car was undamaged at handover and that the damage happened during the rental, so a missing protocol usually hurts the lessor. In other countries, check local rules and your terms, and build your process so you could prove it anyway.

### How long can I keep the rental photos?

Only as long as you need them for a claim. For EU businesses, set a retention period in advance, delete on schedule and keep only photos tied to open disputes.

## Where I'd start

Start with a fixed photo set, taken with the customer present and sent to them on the spot, and a clear rule for after-hours returns. Run it for two months and count disputes and write-offs. Add AI comparison only if reviewing photos becomes the bottleneck, and never let software send a charge without a person approving it.
