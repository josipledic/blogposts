Tuesday morning, four inquiries. A couple wants the truck for their wedding on a Saturday in June: 80 guests, an evening buffet. A street food festival offers a pitch on the same Saturday, 15% of sales, "about 8,000 visitors expected". A software company asks for a Friday summer-party lunch for "around 120 people, budget flexible". And the weekly market slot you've had for two years is still there, as it is every Thursday.

The truck can only be in one place on Saturday. A common way to decide is to answer the message that sounds nicest, or the one that makes you feel guilty. That is how a truck ends up at a rained-out festival on a day that doesn't even cover the staff.

The four offers don't pay in the same way, so gut feeling is a poor judge. Turn each one into a break-even number and a worst case before you reply. That's where I think AI earns its place: reading the messy request, filling in your numbers and putting a clear yes, no or "tell me more" in front of you. Not choosing for you.

## Four kinds of bookings, four ways to get paid

| Booking | How you get paid | Who carries the risk | What to ask first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Private catering (wedding, birthday) | Price per person or a minimum turnover, usually with a deposit | The client, if there is a minimum; you, if there isn't | Guest count by when? Minimum? Deposit? |
| Company lunch or party | Fixed fee or minimum, often against a purchase order | Mostly the company; you, if payment terms are long | Budget per head, who pays, how guests order |
| Weekly market or lunch spot | You sell and pay a pitch fee | You, but small and measurable after a few weeks | Fee, days, footfall in recent weeks |
| Festival or public event | You sell, the organizer takes a flat fee, a share of sales, or both | You: weather, crowd size and competing trucks | Fee model, guarantee, last year's real attendance |

Minimums are common in private catering because a truck costs almost the same to run for 25 guests as for 100. The food truck caterer [holytruck.de](https://holytruck.de/blog/was-kostet-ein-foodtruck/) lists the fixed parts: prep and shopping, team planning, cleaning, vehicle, set-up and standing time.

For public events, a yardstick helps. The [Washington State Food Truck Association](https://wafoodtrucks.org/event-information), a US trade body of truck owners, advises hosts to charge a flat fee or a percentage of gross sales, never both. A percentage should ideally be 10% or lower and never above 15%, and an event taking more than 10% should offer a minimum sales guarantee. That's one association's position, not a rule, and I couldn't find an equivalent guideline for Germany or the UK. Treat it as an opening position.

Read the cancellation clause too. The [published terms of one German street food festival organizer](https://street-food-festival.de/agb) charge a flat stand rent, not a share, but the pattern is worth knowing: 10% of the rent if you cancel up to six weeks ahead, the full rent from six weeks to 21 days ahead, and later a penalty of double the stand fee. A festival "yes" can be expensive to undo, and the better offer for that Saturday tends to arrive after you've said it.

## What the few available numbers say

Hard numbers on which bookings pay best are scarce. It is self-reported and not representative, so read it as a hint.

Now the wedding from the same inbox: 80 guests at an assumed 24 € per head, two people on the truck for about six hours (264 €) plus roughly 150 € for fuel, power and disposables.

| Scenario | Net sales | Organizer's 15% | Food at 32% | Staff and other costs | Left for you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Festival, rained out | 900 € | 135 € | 288 € | 820 € | -343 € |
| Festival, expected | 3,200 € | 480 € | 1,024 € | 820 € | 876 € |
| Festival, strong day | 4,800 € | 720 € | 1,536 € | 820 € | 1,724 € |
| Wedding, 80 guests | 1,920 € | none | 614 € | 414 € | 892 € |

Weight the three festival days 25%, 50% and 25% (a guess) and the festival averages about 780 €. The wedding makes about 890 € and doesn't depend on the weather. Close on paper, but different bets: a calculated risk with upside against a protected job with a ceiling. That's the case for asking the organizer for a guarantee, a lower share or a flat fee. At a 10% share and the same expected sales, the expected day gains 160 €.

I'd draw two hard lines. The assistant never accepts a booking or confirms a date, and it never does the maths itself. A model is good at reading "about 120 people, budget flexible" and a festival PDF. It shouldn't multiply 0.53 into a break-even figure. Keep the formula in a spreadsheet and let the model fill the cells.

## What lands on your phone

The summary I'd aim for, with made-up details:

```text
FESTIVAL INQUIRY, Saturday and Sunday in June
Fee: 15% of net sales, no flat fee. Guarantee: none stated.
Attendance: "about 8,000" (organizer's figure, no past count).
Power: 16 A offered. Water, cancellation terms: not stated.
Officially designated event (festgesetzt): not stated.
Calendar: Saturday already holds a wedding, deposit paid.
Break-even: about 1,550 € a day. Last comparable event: 2,900 €.
Suggested: decline Saturday, ask about Sunday plus last year's
attendance, guarantee, cancellation terms. Draft attached.
```

It shows what's missing, what the calendar says and what the day must earn. The decision stays yours, in two minutes instead of an evening with a three-page PDF.

## What to ask before you say yes

Most missing information is predictable, so ask for it up front.

<Checklist title="Ten questions for every event" items={[
  "Exact times, including set-up and teardown, and where the truck stands",
  "Fee model: flat, share of sales (gross or net of VAT?) or both, and whether there is a guarantee",
  "Last year's actual attendance, not the projection, and how many trucks were booked",
  "Other trucks with a similar menu, and whether you get exclusivity",
  "Power and water: what is provided, what you bring, what it costs",
  "Is the event officially designated as a market or fair? Ask in writing",
  "Who sells alcohol, and who holds the permit?",
  "Cancellation and weather clauses, and what is refunded if the event is called off",
  "Payment terms and deposit for private and company jobs",
  "Guest dietary needs, with a deadline for the final list"
]} />

A form with these as required fields and a one-page rate card remove most of the back-and-forth and cost nothing.

## Paperwork that changes the maths

This is not legal advice, and the rules move. Here is what I could verify in September 2026, for Germany and the US.

**Germany, trading licence.** According to the [IHK Schwarzwald-Baar-Heuberg](https://www.ihk.de/sbh/branchen/tourismus-gastronomie/hotellerie-gastronomie/gruendung-eines-foodtrucks-oder-imbisswagens-7012610), a classic food truck in practice registers as travelling trade and needs a Reisegewerbekarte, which costs 150 to 500 euros depending on the municipality and is valid across Germany. It isn't needed at an officially designated event such as a market or fair, but check beforehand that the event really is designated. That's the "festgesetzt" question in the checklist. The IHK separates travelling from standing trade by initiative: in a standing trade the customer approaches the business, even if only by phone. Whether your pre-booked weddings count as one or the other is a question for your Gewerbeamt.

**Germany, alcohol and seating.** Hospitality law is state law. According to the [IHK Niederbayern](https://www.ihk-niederbayern.de/beratung-und-service/fuer-ihre-branche/tourismus/voraussetzungen-gastgewerbe-reisebranche/verkaufsstaende-speisewagen-und-foodtrucks/), a Bavarian truck offering on-the-spot consumption, for example with standing tables, needs a Gaststättenerlaubnis, and so does serving alcohol. In Baden-Württemberg the permit was replaced by a notification on 1 January 2026, and a temporary operation for a special occasion must be notified two weeks ahead, says the [Landkreis Rastatt](https://www.landkreis-rastatt.de/neues_landesgaststaettenrecht_bw). Ask the Gewerbeamt where each event takes place.

**Germany, food rules.** The IHK Niederbayern page also lists an instruction under the Infection Protection Act from the local health office. For allergens, [Lebensmittelklarheit](https://www.lebensmittelklarheit.de/informationen/allergenkennzeichnung-bei-lebensmitteln-diese-angaben-sind-pflicht) explains that the 14 main allergens must also be disclosed for unpackaged food, orally if a sign says where to ask and written documentation is available. My post on [allergen labelling for canteens](/posts/ai-for-canteens-allergen-labelling) covers the kitchen side.

**United States.** The FDA says food trucks are regulated by [state and local government](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-industry/how-start-food-business). Check each event's location, not just your home base.

## Data protection

Inquiries hold names, phone numbers, addresses and sometimes dietary notes. Allergies and halal or kosher requests can reveal health or religious belief, which the GDPR treats as [special-category data](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/). Collect only what the kitchen needs, keep guest lists out of public chatbots, delete them after the event, and sign a data processing agreement with your AI provider if you serve EU customers.

If the assistant only drafts replies that you send, customers notice nothing. If a chatbot answers them directly on your website or messenger, [Article 50 of the AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) requires telling them it's an AI, and has applied since 2 August 2026.

## What it costs

My rough assumptions, not a quote: a booking form and a break-even sheet cost nothing to 15 € a month. An assistant that reads inquiries and drafts replies adds about 20 to 40 € a month at 30 to 60 inquiries. Setup is a day or two: your rules, a rate card and reply templates, tested on last season's inquiries. One avoided loss like the rained-out festival above (minus 343 €) roughly covers a year of tool costs.

## When you don't need AI

With five inquiries a month, a spreadsheet, a form and a rate card do the job. So does a reputation check: before signing up for a festival, message two trucks that did it last year. No model knows whether the 8,000 visitors showed up.

AI starts to pay when inquiries pile up in peak season, arrive on four channels or come as long PDFs. For private jobs, [replying within the hour](/posts/ai-for-catering-inquiries) is its own topic, and [how venues handle inquiries](/posts/ai-for-event-venues-inquiries) shows the organizer's side of the table.

## Questions food truck owners ask

### Are festivals worth it for a small food truck?

Sometimes. Work out break-even sales first and compare it with what a comparable day earned. In the German survey above, festival-focused trucks sat mostly in the lower profit classes. If the organizer takes a share, push for a lower percentage, a flat fee or a guarantee.

### How much should I charge for a private event?

Start from your costs: food, staff, travel, set-up and the prep day. Then set a minimum turnover that works even with fewer guests than hoped, plus a deposit and a deadline for the final guest count.

### Do I need a Reisegewerbekarte for a food truck in Germany?

Often yes: the IHK says registering travelling trade is the rule for a classic food truck. Officially designated events such as markets and fairs are the exception, and pre-booked private jobs may be treated differently, so ask your Gewerbeamt.

### Can AI decide which events I should accept?

It can prepare the decision: extract the facts, flag gaps and fill your sheet. It can't know the organizer's reputation. The yes or no is yours.

## Where I'd start

Know your break-even before you reply to anything. Take an event only if a bad day costs less than a good day earns, and ask for a guarantee whenever the organizer takes a share. Build the form and the spreadsheet first. Add the assistant when the inbox outgrows them.
