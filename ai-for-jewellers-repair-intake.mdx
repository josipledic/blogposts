Picture a customer, Anna, handing over her grandmother's 18-carat ring to be made two sizes bigger. The assistant writes on a paper envelope: "Ring, yellow gold, 52 to 54, stone loose?" Anna gets a stub, the ring goes into a tray, and the tray goes to the workshop.

Three weeks later she collects it and frowns. There's a scratch along the band. The centre stone, she says, looked bigger before. The jeweller remembers nothing specific, the envelope says "stone loose?", and nobody photographed anything. It's her word against a question mark.

Neither side is lying, and that's what makes these disputes expensive. The repair itself may have been perfect. What was missing is a record of what walked in the door. A repair counter is where a customer's most personal property leaves the room, and it's often documented on the back of an envelope.

This post is about fixing that record: photos, a voice note, a structured ticket and a customer signature, with AI doing the typing and the jeweller doing the judging.

## What the envelope says, and what a dispute asks

Here is a typical paper repair slip next to the questions that come up when something goes wrong.

| What the slip says | What a dispute asks | What would have answered it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Ring, gold, resize" | Which metal and fineness? Was there a hallmark? | Photo of the hallmark, weight on the scale |
| "Stone loose?" | Which stone, how loose, already chipped? | Close-ups of the stone and every prong, plus the customer's own words |
| Nothing | Was the band scratched before? | Dated overview photos from every side, ticket number in frame |
| "Ready in 2 weeks" | Which price and date were agreed? | Estimate, deadline and pickup terms the customer signed |
| "Anna, 0171 555 0142" | Where is the ring now? | Status: at the counter, in the workshop, at an outside specialist |

None of the right-hand column is exotic. It just isn't captured at the moment it's cheapest to capture: while the customer is standing there.

## Where repair disputes come from

I'd sort them into three kinds.

**Pre-existing damage.** Scratches, dents, chipped stones, a prong that was already worn. By pickup day, memory has rewritten the starting condition. Some stones are also fragile in ways nobody sees. GIA notes that [some estimates put the share of fracture-filled emeralds at 90 percent or more](https://www.gia.edu/emerald-care-cleaning), that heat can extend existing fractures, and that ultrasonic cleaning and steam are risky for them. If an emerald comes back cracked, the cause may be the workshop, or a fracture that was already there. A line at intake that says "emerald, fracture-filled, no ultrasonic, no steam" protects the stone and the shop.

**Lost or changed stones.** Loose stones are the classic. Jewelers Mutual, a US jewellery insurer, says more than half of its claims are partial losses, and [losing a stone from a ring is one of the more common cases](https://www.jewelers.org/buying-jewelry/jewelry-repair-and-care/jewelry-insurance), as reported by Jewelers of America. Those are wearers' claims, not workshop errors, but they show how often settings fail. A loose stone that was noted and photographed at intake is a known risk. One that falls out in the workshop with no record is an argument.

**Lost track.** Pieces move between counter, goldsmith and sometimes an outside specialist for laser welding or watch work. Every handover is a place to lose the record, and every "where's my ring?" call costs staff time.

The trade is large enough for this to matter. German jewellery and watch retail turned over [€5.26 billion in 2024](https://bv-schmuck-uhren.de/deutsche-juweliere-werte-steigen-stueckzahlen-nicht/) at consumer prices, the latest figure I could find. The 2019 tax statistics, the count I could verify, showed [7,179 retail businesses](https://www.bv-juweliere.de/Aktuell/Presseinformation/Strukturveraenderung-im-Handel-mit-Uhren-und-Schmuck), mostly small and medium-sized. Metal values are high too: gold traded at about $4,180 a troy ounce on 2 October 2026, down from a record near $5,600 in January, [according to Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/commodity/gold). By my arithmetic, a 5 g ring in 18-carat gold holds about 3.75 g of fine gold, roughly $500 of metal before any stone or workmanship. Small pieces, real money.

## What the law expects at the counter

This is not legal advice, and rules differ by country. Three points come up again and again.

**Who has to prove what.** In the UK, every service contract carries an implied term that the trader must [perform the service with reasonable care and skill](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/49). German courts are explicit about who has to prove what. As a [legal explainer on JuraForum](https://www.juraforum.de/lexikon/schaden-durch-handwerker-beweislast) sets it out, the customer has to show the breach, the damage and the causal link. Once they have, [§ 280 BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__280.html) puts the burden on the repairer to show it wasn't at fault. The same source notes that a repairer can be cleared when the customer insisted on a risky method despite a clear warning, and that after acceptance the burden of proving a defect generally shifts to the customer. In either country, a dated record of the condition at handover and a signed warning do the heavy lifting.

**Insurance.** In a 2016 ruling (VII ZR 107/15), Germany's Federal Court of Justice held that a jeweller [generally need not insure customers' jewellery against theft or robbery](https://www.bundesgerichtshof.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/DE/2016/2016094.html) while it's in for repair, but must say so if the pieces are exceptionally valuable or insurance is customary in the trade. The case involved jewellery worth up to €2,930, and the court sent the question of what's customary back to the lower court. The practical lesson is to state in writing what is and isn't covered. Check your own policy too: standard business liability cover usually excludes damage to property in your custody and needs an add-on, [according to an insurance comparison site](https://versicherungsvergleiche.de/fragen/was-sind-obhutsschaeden-in-der-betriebshaftpflicht/), while [specialist jewellers' policies](https://www.asscompact.de/nachrichten/wo-doch-alles-gold-ist-was-gl%C3%A4nzt-absicherung-f%C3%BCr-juweliere-co) can include working damage to watches and jewellery. Ask your broker.

**Estimates and uncollected work.** If a job will cost substantially more than quoted, German law ([§ 649 BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__649.html)) requires you to tell the customer without delay. For pieces nobody collects, don't sell or scrap them. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the [Torts (Interference with Goods) Act 1977](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1977/32/schedule/1) sets out a notice procedure: a written request to collect, then a notice of intention to sell sent by registered or recorded-delivery post, with at least three months' notice where money is owed. In Germany the repairer's lien ([§ 647 BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__647.html)) is enforced through a formal sale (a threat of sale, a one-month wait, a public auction), and gold items can't be sold below their melt value ([§ 1240 BGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__1240.html)). So the pickup deadline and the customer's agreement to written notices belong on the ticket from day one.

## The intake I'd build

The same logic as [damage checks at a car rental counter](/posts/ai-for-car-rental-damage-checks) applies: record the starting condition with the customer present, in a fixed routine, before anything is touched.

<Flow title="From the counter to the workshop and back" caption="AI types and files. The jeweller looks, decides and signs off. The customer signs what was seen.">
<Step title="Look at it together" who="Staff and customer" tone="human" time="3 to 5 min">Take the standard photos with the ticket number in frame, then speak a 30-second voice note: what the customer asks for, what they say is wrong, what you can see.</Step>
<Step title="Draft the ticket" who="AI" tone="ai" time="under 1 min">Turns voice note and photos into a structured ticket: item, requested work, metal and stones as stated, visible condition, open questions. Flags missing photos.</Step>
<Step title="Check and decide" who="Jeweller" tone="human" time="2 min">Corrects every condition remark against the piece, adds what the loupe shows, flags fragile stones, sets the price range, and decides whether to take the job and how.</Step>
<Step title="Customer reads and signs" who="Customer" time="1 min">The ticket shows photos, condition remarks, estimate, pickup deadline and insurance note. The customer signs on paper or on a tablet.</Step>
<Step title="Job sheet and status" who="AI" tone="ai">Builds a job sheet for the workshop with photos and warnings, and drafts short status messages at set points. A changed estimate always goes to the jeweller first.</Step>
<Step title="Compare at pickup" who="Staff and customer" tone="human" time="2 min">The piece is checked against the intake photos together, then handed over and signed for.</Step>
</Flow>

Two decisions do most of the work.

**The jeweller owns every condition remark.** The model drafts "small scratch on the underside of the band, left of the hallmark" from what was said and shown. It doesn't add what nobody mentioned, and it never declares a piece undamaged. A person reads the draft with the piece in hand.

**The ticket lives in the system you already have.** Jeweller-specific systems such as [PrismaNote](https://www.prismanote.de/Reparatur-system) and [ETRON onRetail](https://www.etron.de/kassensysteme-juweliere/) in Germany, or Bravo and The Edge in the US, already hold repair tickets and photos. The AI layer fills the fields from the voice note and drafts the messages. The system stays the record.

The photos are where most shops are inconsistent, so I'd fix the routine in writing.

<Checklist title="The standard set at the counter" items={[
  "Overview of the whole piece, front and back, on a plain mat with the ticket number in frame",
  "Every stone from above and from the side, and every prong or setting close up",
  "Hallmarks and maker's marks, and the weight on the scale",
  "Existing damage: scratches, dents, chips, loose parts, earlier repairs, each photographed with a pointer",
  "Function: does the clasp close, is the safety catch there, does the watch run",
  "The customer's own words on what is wrong and what they want, quoted",
  "Stones and metal as the customer states them, recorded as stated and not verified",
  "Fragile stones and any method warning (heat, ultrasonic, steam) written on the ticket",
  "Pickup deadline, estimate and insurance note shown to the customer before they sign"
]} />

## Describe, don't identify

<Callout title="The ticket records what was said and seen, never what a stone is" tone="warning">
A model can write "colourless round stone, about 3 mm, four prongs, customer states diamond, not tested". It must never write "diamond", and it must never put a value on the piece. According to GIA, lab-grown and natural diamonds are essentially the same chemically and optically, so traditional gem observation and instruments cannot tell them apart. That takes a gemological laboratory, and a photo can't do it either. Valuations for insurance come from an appraiser, not from a repair ticket. Keep the model in the role of a careful scribe.
</Callout>

GIA's [FAQ on identifying laboratory-grown diamonds](https://www.gia.edu/gia-faq-analysis-grading-identify-laboratory-grown-diamonds) says the same. The caution covers photo review too. A model can tell you a picture is blurry or that the hallmark isn't visible. It can't reliably judge a hairline chip, and a missed one on a signed ticket is worse than none.

## When a paper slip is enough

If you take in five repairs a week, a printed slip with a drawing of the piece to mark up, a numbered bag, and phone photos saved in a folder named after the ticket number gets you most of the way. No model needed.

AI starts to earn its place with higher volume, several people taking repairs, an outside workshop, or customers who keep phoning for status. It's good at turning a spoken minute into a clean ticket, and at the repetitive messages. The slip, the photos and the signature do the legal work either way.

## What careful intake costs

The hardware is a phone, a neutral mat and good light, which most shops have. The running cost is staff minutes plus whatever the transcription and drafting service charges.

Set that against one remade ring or replaced stone, which can easily cost a few hundred euros, plus the review the customer leaves. On pure arithmetic, the intake pays for itself only if disputes are frequent. The stronger case isn't in the box: fewer "where is it?" calls, and customers who watch you handle their piece with care. If disputes are rare, start with the paper version.

## Customer data and privacy

Photos and tickets linked to a named customer are personal data. A voice note sent to a transcription service means a processor, so under GDPR you need a data processing agreement (Article 28) and a clear view of where the data is stored. Tell customers on the ticket what you photograph and why: to document the condition of the item and to handle any later claim. Photograph the piece, not the person, and don't copy ID for a repair.

Retention is a judgement call, so ask your adviser. Under German law the regular limitation period is [three years](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__195.html), counted from the [end of the year](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__199.html) in which the claim arose and became known, and defect claims on repair work expire after [two years](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__634a.html) from acceptance. I'd keep tickets and photos until three years after the year of pickup, then delete them.

## Questions jewellers ask about repair tickets

### Is a photo enough to prove what condition a piece was in?

It helps a lot, but it isn't magic. A dated photo with the ticket number in frame, plus condition remarks the customer signed, is much stronger than either alone.

### Can AI tell me whether a stone is real, or what a piece is worth?

No. Even specialists need laboratory instruments to separate natural from lab-grown diamonds, and valuation is an appraiser's job. Let the ticket record what the customer says, labelled as stated.

### How long should I keep intake photos and tickets?

Long enough to cover the period in which a claim can still be made. In Germany that means about three years after the year of pickup. Check the rule where you trade.

### What do I do with repairs nobody collects?

Don't sell them on your own. Put the pickup deadline and the notice terms on the ticket, send the written reminders the law requires, and follow the formal sale procedure where you are.

### Do I have to insure a customer's piece while it's in the workshop?

In Germany the Federal Court of Justice says not as a general rule, with exceptions for exceptionally valuable pieces or where insurance is customary. Either way, tell the customer in writing what's covered, and check your own policy for custody damage.

## Where I'd start

Photograph before you touch, write down what you can see rather than what you think the stone is, and have the customer sign what you both looked at. Run the paper version for the next ten repairs, then add voice-to-ticket once the habit holds.
