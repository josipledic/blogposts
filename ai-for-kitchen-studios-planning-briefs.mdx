Picture a couple in a kitchen studio on a Saturday afternoon. They spend two hours with a planner. They talk about an island, a tall larder unit, the induction hob they saw at a friend's house, and the fact that the dishwasher has to be on the left because he is left-handed. The planner sketches, nods, takes a few notes and promises a detailed quote by Thursday.

On Thursday the planner opens the notes. They read "island?? 120 cm max", "no wall units on the window side", "hob: induction, she wants to keep the old extractor". Was the 120 cm the depth or the width? Did they say the extractor stays, or that they would think about it? Nobody remembers exactly, including the couple. Six weeks later the fitter arrives, and the answer becomes very expensive.

I think of this as a brief problem, not a design problem. Planning a kitchen takes real skill, and the planner does it well. The information that feeds the design often lives in one person's head and a few scribbled lines. That is the part AI can help with.

Most of those problems are not design failures. They are information failures: a measurement that was wrong, an appliance that changed after the quote, a water connection that was not where everybody assumed.

## Where information leaks out of a consultation

A typical consultation collects four kinds of information, and each one leaks in its own way.

**Facts about the room.** Wall lengths, ceiling height, window and door positions, radiators, the location of water supply, drain, sockets and the extractor duct. These get measured once, often by someone other than the planner, and written on a sheet that may or may not travel with the file.

**Facts about the equipment.** Which oven, which hob, which fridge, which dishwasher, and whether the customer brings old appliances. One change here (a wider fridge, a different hob) changes cabinet widths and sometimes electrical requirements.

**Wishes and habits.** Left-handed, two people cooking at once, a dog that sleeps in the kitchen, never wants to see the bins. These are the details that make the kitchen feel right, and they are the first to vanish from memory.

**Money and time.** The budget, what is negotiable, and the date when the old kitchen has to be gone because the move-in is fixed.

The planner remembers the gist of all four. A brief is what you get when the gist is written down in the same structure every time, reviewed by both sides, and attached to the order.

## From recording to brief

Here is the flow I'd build. Nothing in it touches the planner's CAD work.

<Flow title="From a two-hour conversation to a signed-off brief" caption="The assistant structures what was said. The planner decides what goes into the plan, and the customer confirms the brief before any quote is final.">
<Step title="Ask for consent, then record" who="Planner" tone="human" time="1 min">The planner explains that the conversation is recorded and summarised for the project file, and records only with a clear yes.</Step>
<Step title="Consultation as usual" who="Planner and customer" tone="human" time="2 hours">Sketches and CAD drawing happen exactly as today. The recording runs in the background.</Step>
<Step title="Transcribe and structure" who="AI" tone="ai" time="minutes">The transcript is sorted into the brief template: room facts, equipment, wishes, budget, dates, decisions, and open questions.</Step>
<Step title="Flag gaps and conflicts" who="AI" tone="ai">Missing measurements, contradictions ("wants a 60 cm oven, but also a 45 cm tall unit"), and anything said once and never confirmed.</Step>
<Step title="Planner reviews and corrects" who="Planner" tone="human" time="10 min">The planner fixes errors, deletes what should not be in the file, and answers what they can.</Step>
<Step title="Customer confirms by email" who="Customer" tone="human">The customer gets the brief in plain language, with the open questions listed. A reply of "yes" or corrections becomes part of the order.</Step>
</Flow>

The last step matters most. A brief nobody has confirmed is just a nicer set of notes. The customer's reply turns "I thought we agreed the extractor stays" into something you can point to.

## What the brief contains

I'd keep it to a page or two, with the same headings every time, so that the planner, the measuring technician and the fitter all know where to look.

| Section | What goes in | Who confirms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Room facts | Wall lengths, ceiling height, windows, doors, radiators, connection positions, with the source of each number (measured, estimated, from the customer's plan) | Measuring technician |
| Appliances | Model or at least type and width, who supplies it, and any special connection such as a three-phase hob | Customer and planner |
| Must-haves and never-wants | Left-handed, no wall units by the window, keep the old table, child-safe handles | Customer |
| Budget and priorities | Ceiling, what to spend on first, what to cut first | Customer |
| Dates | Move-in, removal of the old kitchen, delivery window, builder or plumber dependencies | Planner |
| Open questions | Every gap, each with an owner and a due date | Planner |

The "source of each number" column deserves a second look. A wall length the customer estimated by pacing it out is not the same as one a technician measured with a laser. The brief should say which it is. Quite a few complaints start with an estimate that hardened into a fact.

## The site facts checklist

The transcript only captures what people said. What nobody said is worse. A short checklist, filled in by the customer before the first appointment or by the planner on the spot, catches the facts that are easy to overlook. The assistant can compare the filled list against the conversation and point out what is still missing.

None of this needs AI. A printed sheet does the job. The AI helps only when the conversation and the sheet disagree.

## Consent, recording and data protection

Recording a consultation needs care, and the rules differ by country. In Germany, recording a private conversation without the consent of the people involved can be a crime under [section 201 of the Criminal Code](https://dejure.org/gesetze/StGB/201.html), with up to three years in prison or a fine. Some US states require all parties to agree as well, as a [legal overview of AI recording and transcription](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/employment-law-watch/102ls2n/the-legality-of-ai-powered-recording-and-transcription/) explains. Consent to recording is also not the same as consent to AI processing, so say both.

In the EU, the GDPR applies to the recording and the transcript. Tell customers what you record, why, and how long you keep it. Delete the audio once the brief is confirmed, and keep the brief. If an outside service transcribes or summarises the conversation, you need a data processing agreement with it and should check where the data is stored.

Kitchens have a personal side. People talk about budgets, children, health problems that affect the layout, and sometimes a divorce. The assistant should capture what is needed for the design and leave the rest out of the brief. Give the customer the option of "switch it off for now", and respect it.

## Where AI is the wrong tool

Measurements. A model does not measure a room, and it must never guess a missing dimension. The technician's laser and a proper survey stay the source for every number that ends up in the plan.

The plan itself. Kitchens are drawn and priced in planning software that knows the manufacturers' catalogues. Studios commonly use tools such as [Carat, Winner, KPS or eOPUS](https://www.kuechendesk.de/), and [Winner Flex from Cyncly](https://www.cyncly.com/products/winner-flex) is one of the cloud-based options. A language model should not invent article numbers, module widths or prices. It feeds the planner's work, it doesn't replace the catalogue.

A simpler fix may be enough. If your studio has four planners, and each already writes thorough notes, a standard intake sheet and a confirmation email after every consultation solve most of the problem for free. Try that first. AI earns its place when consultations are long, when several people touch one project, or when the notes vary wildly from planner to planner.

One more caution: [Stiftung Warentest](https://www.test.de/Einbaukueche-Kuechenkauf-So-klappen-Kauf-Montage-Reklamation-5715545-0/) advises customers to put complaints in writing and not to sign an installation protocol for things they haven't checked. A studio that documents its own decisions well is on stronger ground in those disputes too.

## A small pilot

Take five consultations over a month. Record them with consent, produce the briefs, have the planners correct them, and send them to the customers. Then compare at the fitting stage: did any surprises appear that the brief had flagged, and were there any the brief missed? If the planners spend more time correcting the brief than they saved, change the template before you go further.

## Questions kitchen studio owners ask

### Can AI design the kitchen?

Not in the sense that matters for a purchase. It can suggest ideas, but the plan has to be built in your planning software with real modules, real dimensions and real prices. Treat any generated image as inspiration, never as a drawing the customer might expect you to deliver.

### Do I have to tell customers I'm recording?

Yes, and get a clear yes before you start. In Germany, recording without consent can be a criminal offence, and under the GDPR you also have to explain what happens with the data. A single sentence at the start, repeated in the confirmation email, covers it.

### What if the customer says no?

Then you work as you do today. The brief can be written from the planner's own notes or dictated after the meeting, and the same template applies. The recording is a convenience, not a condition.

### Can the assistant write the quote?

I'd keep it away from prices. The quote comes from the planning software and your price lists. The assistant can make sure the quote matches the brief, listing every appliance and wish that appears in one and not the other, and leave the numbers to the software.

## My rule of thumb

If it was said in the consultation, it goes into the brief. If it is not in the brief, the customer has not confirmed it. If a number is not measured, the brief says so.
